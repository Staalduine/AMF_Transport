\begin{abstract}
 
The symbiosis between plants and arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi is fundamental to terrestrial ecosystems as it is responsible for large flows of nutrients on a planetary scale.
In this symbiosis, two distinct organisms trade phosphorus (P) for energy in the form of carbon (C).
However, the mechanism behind the bidirectional transport of resources within their microscopic hyphae has not been entirely elucidated.
We imaged transport dynamics of nutrient flows in high resolution in different positions in growing networks, and also perturbed flows through section cuts and osmotic shocks.
Our data suggest that carbon, as lipid, travels towards the growing tips within a condensate that molecular motors carry in one direction.
Our findings also suggest a tight coupling between network growth, internal carbon (lipid) transport dynamics, and bidirectional fluid movement within the network.
In an open pipe system, moving this condensate towards the tips pushes cytoplasmic fluid back towards the root, a backflow that may carry phosphorus.
Our model provides an intuitive understanding for the observed proportionality in resource exchange between the two organisms.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

The symbiosis between arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi and their host plants has existed for 400 million years~\cite{remy_four_1994,brundrett_evolutionary_2018}.
For the symbiosis to endure this long, robust mechanisms must be in place to ensure its stability.
Indeed, symbiosis can be a many to many relationship, which means that the plant can interact with multiple independent fungal colonies and one fungal network can interact with multiple plant hosts~\cite{kiers_reciprocal_2011,walder_mycorrhizal_2012}.
Without selective orientation of nutrients towards partners that engage in a proportional effort to provide nutrients, ``free ride'' plant or fungal phenotypes can emerge that reap the benefits of symbiosis while avoiding costs~\cite{kiers_reciprocal_2011,bever_preferential_2009,walder_regulation_2015,noe_mycorrhizal_2018,kiers_misconceptions_2016}. 

The fungal partner forms a network in the soil using plant derived carbon.
It absorbs phosphorus from the soil, which is then transported back to the plant.
This means that carbon must constantly be shipped to the growing edge of the colony away from the plant, while phosphorus needs to be transported back in the other direction.
Like other coenocytic fungi, AM fungi have aseptate hyphae, meaning that the organism can be considered a fully open pipe system, while they are growing.
The mechanisms that could explain such bidirectional transport in a network of open pipes are so far unknown.

Our group had previously estimated that, when a single AM fungus was attached to a single plant, the fungal use of carbon for growth was proportional to the flux of phosphorus towards the plant~\cite{bisot_carbon-phosphorous_2025}.
In addition, it has been observed in the past that, when a single AM network was connected to two different plant hosts that differed in their carbon supply, more phosphorus was directed towards the plant that provided more carbon~\cite{kiers_reciprocal_2011}. 

Past research has quantified the flow speed in both directions in networks of different ages~\cite{oyarte_galvez_travelling-wave_2025}.
However, without staining the lipids and/or phosphorus, it is difficult to understand the organization of resource flow across the network and the underlying transport mechanisms.
Resource specific staining can shed light on the distribution of resource flux within the network.
For example, using NileRed staining and two-photon microscopy, it was found that lipid density varies along the hyphae, from about 24\% of the hyphal volume near the root to about 0.5\% at the front, and that lipid bodies move in both directions, mostly with the cytoplasmic stream~\cite{bago_translocation_2002}.
The role of microtubule tracks in this movement has been proposed, and microtubules have been imaged throughout the network~\cite{timonen_microtubules_2001}, but to our knowledge the link between microtubules and lipid transport has never been established.

Flow speeds along the hyphae are modulated with time and location within the network~\cite{oyarte_galvez_travelling-wave_2025}.
This can be interpreted through a traffic analogy inspired by the observed correlation between betweenness centrality, a typical transport network metric, and flow speeds.
The betweenness centrality is the number of shortest paths between pairs of nodes in a network that cross a particular network edge.
Here, we set the root and tip nodes as the set of source and target nodes.
However, this correlation lacks a direct physical basis that explains how such a relationship between global metrics and flows could emerge mechanically.
Because AM fungi are coenocytic, the colony can be represented as a hydraulic network of connected pipes with varying radii.
This hydraulic coupling between all elements of the network could generate spatial correlation in ways that can be physically framed.
Such a mechanical framework, validated against measurements, for growing AM colonies is still missing.

What mechanisms explain the bidirectional transport of resources in the mycorrhizal colony and its modulation over space and time?
How can the transport mechanisms in place maintain a form of bidirectional transport in which the magnitudes of resource flux in opposite directions are maintained proportional? 
To answer these questions, we acquired image data at two different spatial and temporal resolutions to quantify the flow speed in specific edges of growing networks.
At high spatial and temporal resolution, we acquired bright-field videos of transport dynamics within single hyphal edges over multiple days.
Using NileRed, a neutral lipid-specific fluorescent dye, we also characterized and quantified the movement of carbon in the form of lipids along these same edges.
At low spatial and temporal resolution, we obtained time-lapse images of growing networks that were transformed into their equivalent mathematical graph.
In this way, we can relate the growth of the network with the measured flow speeds observed in the same network, allowing us to explain high-resolution observations both mechanistically and quantitatively.
Additionally, a small number of perturbation experiments were performed to characterize each flow direction.
From these data and findings, we construct a minimal model that explains the observed bidirectional transport of resources in the colony and its modulation over space and time.

\section*{Results}
\namedsubsection{Asymmetric bidirectional speeds}{subsec:res_speeds}

Fungal networks (\textit{Rhizophagus irregularis}) grow through the extension of fungal tips away from the root of the host plant (\textit{Daucus carota}, Methods, \nameref{subsec:strains}, \cite{oyarte_galvez_travelling-wave_2025}).
Over time, these elongated hyphae develop into a branched mycelial structure.
The network's topological context therefore varies along the length of a hypha.
Close to a growing tip, the network is more tree-like: about 45\% of the edges within 1~mm of a tip are bridges, that is, not part of any loop, versus 10--13\% of the edges further than 2~mm from a tip (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}A; SI Fig.~\ref{fig:tree_like_share}).
Moving away from the tip, more growing hyphae depend on the same runner hypha, and anastomosis (fusion between a hyphal tip and a hyphal segment) creates loops, which give many alternative paths from the tip towards the root~\cite{oyarte_galvez_travelling-wave_2025}.

We asked how this change in topological context affected the speeds of the flows in the two directions along a hypha: towards the growing tip (tipward) and towards the root (rootward), as defined by the growth direction of the network.
We imaged flows at high resolution in 731 bright-field videos along the hyphae of five colonies (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:video_positions}) and, mostly at the same positions, in 625 videos of NileRed fluorescence; for the example network of Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}A--C, the videos were taken over three consecutive days.
A measurement is the analysis of a single edge at one point in time.
We chose runner hyphae, the main routes to the root and the edges of high betweenness centrality~\cite{kirkley_betweenness_2018}, where the tipward and rootward flows could be distinguished by eye (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_video_selection}; SI Fig.~\ref{fig:bc_selection}). 

In undisturbed hyphae (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_lid}), the two flows are superimposed in the video and move in straight lines at nearly constant speed, so that in a kymograph every moving particle leaves a straight diagonal streak, with no sign of random or back-and-forth movement, except near the tips (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}E; SI Fig.~\ref{fig:kymograph_examples}).
In the field of view, the directed movement exceeds diffusion by one to  two orders of magnitude, so the slope of a streak is its speed (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_peclet}).
Each streak gives one speed, and each kymograph was reduced to a set of tipward and rootward speeds (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:speed_flux_extraction_real_data}).
In the histogram of the extracted velocities from a video, the tipward velocities (positive) and the rootward velocities (negative) each form a peak.
We took the median of each of the two groups as the speed of that direction in that video (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}E). 
The tipward speeds slow down near the tip, but stay close to 3~$\mu$m/s further away (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}D).
Rootward speeds generally increase with distance from the tip (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}D), and have a wide range: the median interquartile range of a hypha's speeds is 1.6~$\mu$m/s rootward, against 0.6~$\mu$m/s tipward (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:speed_iqr}).
This result was confirmed by repeating a similar experiment on various plates imaging 15 sessions over 5 plates showing a consistent phenomenology (Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries} A, B).

We asked if the asymmetry in the directional speeds could be due to the difference in cell components that would be moving in the two directions, which could inform the mechanism of movement.
Lipids are an integral part of growth within the network, as they are the main form of carbon that the plant provides to the fungus~\cite{luginbuehl_fatty_2017}. 
We imaged these lipids with NileRed fluorescent dye~\cite{bago_tracking_2002} and compared the fluorescence videos to bright-field videos of the same positions. At the resolution of the fluorescence videos (0.14~$\mu$m per pixel, 0.5 or 1~s per frame, 10 to 20~s per video), we saw no evidence of lipid particles randomly switching direction, as they would in the random walk underlying diffusive motion (Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}C). Overall, it is clear that lipids form a subset of the observed flows in these videos (Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}C), where the bright-field video shows a balanced distribution of tipward and rootward flows, and the lipid video can have a different distribution. In edges where both tipward and rootward flows were detected, the direction bias of detected elements was calculated as the share of the speed-weighted moving signal going to the tip (Methods, \nameref{subsec:direction_bias}). Most bright-field videos show a balanced distribution of tipward and rootward flows, with a median direction bias of 0.49 (Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}D). Lipid videos also mostly show a balanced distribution of tipward and rootward flows, with a broader tail towards tipward flows. From this we can state that lipids appear in both flows, but are more likely to be found in the tipward flow than in the rootward flow (Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}D). Due to speed ceiling limits, this had to be done on a subset of slower-moving positions in the network.

In the past, pressure gradients and molecular motors have been suggested to be potential mechanisms driving flows in fungal hyphae~\cite{steinberg_cell_2017,kokkoris_mycelial_2026}. 
It is also important to discuss other potential mechanisms that could drive flows in fungal hyphae, and to evaluate whether our data support or contradict each of them. 
Diffusion as a mechanism could be present close to the tips, as that is where we observe the most random movement of particles (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:kymograph_examples}, kymograph 4), but the directed, unbroken flow in the rest of the network is not consistent with diffusion alone. 
Singular mass flow, as suggested by previous work~\cite{heaton_growth-induced_2010,lew_mass_2005,lew_how_2011}, is also unlikely to be the main driver of flows in our system, as it cannot explain the presence of two opposing streams at similar speeds in the same hypha (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_mass_flow}). 
Peristaltic flow has been observed and studied in \textit{Physarum}~\cite{alim_mechanism_2017}, but involves flow reversals that are not observed in our system when atmospheric pressure and moisture are kept constant (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_lid}).

The difference in flow behaviour, both within an edge and along an edge, with distance to tip suggests that flow in each direction could be associated with a different mechanism.
An ideal experiment to distinguish these two potential drivers would be to modify the pressure gradient across the network and see how it affects flows.
This experiment has been performed in the past by creating an extracellular osmotic gradient~\cite{lew_mass_2005}.
Following a similar approach, we added a drop of dissolved NaCl solution (200 mOsm) (Methods, \nameref{subsec:perturbations}) to the base of the network a few centimeters behind the growing front and imaged near the growth edge (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}A).
We expected that this would create a low turgor pressure within the hyphae at the location of the drop, shifting the pressure gradient within the network, and therefore changing flows internal to the hyphae.
We observed that the flow speed in the tip direction was almost unaffected by the addition of the salt solution  (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}A);  fluorescence imaging of the lipids showed that their movement in the tip direction was also unaffected (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:salt_shock}D).
By contrast, the flow speed in the root direction was approximately increased 4.3 times and relaxed in half a minute.

Past work proposed that plant host transpiration could be the driving force explaining the flow of resources towards the root~\cite{kikuchi_aquaporin-mediated_2016}.
Although the plant in our root-organ culture does not produce leaves and, therefore, does not evapotranspirate, we tested whether the connection to the plant was necessary for the flows in the two directions to be maintained.
In one network, we imaged a runner hypha at multiple positions from root to tip to measure the velocity profile along its length.
We then severed the network from the root with a scalpel by cutting along its entire bottom (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}B) and recorded the same positions again, in the first half hour after the cut and then again in the second half hour.
Flows continued in both directions.
About 20~min after the cut the rootward stream slowed by about a fifth (median ratio 0.78 per trail edge), while the tipward speed stayed at about 3~$\mu$m/s (ratio 0.96; Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}B).
By 50~min the rootward stream had recovered near the tip but not far from it (SI, \nameref{sec:si_perturbations}), and the asymmetry between the two directions remained throughout.
This matches earlier work showing that flows can still be active for a year without a plant host~\cite{kleinCytoplasmicFlowDynamics2026}.
A connection to the root is therefore not needed for either flow, which rules out transpiration by the plant as their driver.

To further focus on the range of a potential driving force, we wanted to know what the minimal size of the subgraph was that was necessary to conserve flows in the two directions.
We made targeted cuts to isolate small segments from the network and image flows at sufficient distance from the cut-induced stress (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}C).
In patches of network as small as only a few millimeters in total length, we still observed flows in the two directions (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}C).
We observed that the flow speed in both directions was affected, slowing down a bit, but the flow never stopped (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}C).
These experiments demonstrate that the mechanical origin of flows in the two directions does not require long range connectivity of the network to operate (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_segment_size}). 

\namedsubsection{Flow model}{subsec:res_hypothesis}

For tipward flows, evidence points to a mechanism that is insensitive to pressure gradients and capable of maintaining a speed that is modulated by both hyphal diameter and distance to tip, but is otherwise robust to perturbations (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_forward_flow}).
The lack of response to osmotic shocks, the lack of response to root cuts, and little response to segment isolation all suggest that the mechanism is not pressure driven and that there is something local to the hypha that is responsible for the tipward flows.
We have ruled out diffusion, mass flow, and peristaltic streaming in the previous section as drivers of network-wide flows, and the evidence is consistent with cargo carried by molecular motors along microtubules, which are present throughout AM fungal hyphae~\cite{timonen_microtubules_2001}.

This hypothesis is supported by the characteristics of its likely cargo, a lipid-containing condensate.
In some way, the lipid droplets in it have to arrive at the tips to facilitate growth.
Lipid droplets are believed to be transported by molecular motors in AM fungi~\cite{bago_translocation_2002}, it has been shown that other fungal species transport lipids along microtubules~\cite{guimaraes_peroxisomes_2015, lin_active_2016}, and the speed of the tipward flows is consistent with the range of speeds of molecular motors~\cite{steinberg_cell_2017}.
The tipward flows move at a steady speed, in a straight line. 
However, flows towards the root are sensitive to pressure changes from osmotic shocks (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}A).
While they are affected by segment isolation, they are still able to flow, indicating that whatever is driving the flows in this direction is not dependent on long range connectivity of the network.
The flows towards the root are also more variable in speed.
This suggests that the mechanism driving these flows is different from the mechanism driving the tipward flows and is likely pressure driven.

We synthesize the above results into a minimal model that explains the observed bidirectional transport of resources in the network and its modulation over space and time.
Namely that motors carry a condensate of lipid droplets in water towards the tips, and that this movement of volume creates a local pressure gradient that drives a backflow towards the root (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}ABC).
This model is consistent with the observed flows in both directions, and with the section cut and osmotic shock perturbation experiments that we performed.

As this condensate moves towards the tips throughout the aseptate mycelium, it could drag cytoplasmic fluid along by viscous friction and, because no water can leave the hyphae, push an equal volume of water back towards the root.
As such, the condensate, a water-oil-like emulsion of lipid droplets of higher viscosity, forms a layer that uniformly coats the internal surface of the hyphal pipe.
We assume the speed profile of the condensate layer in its cross-section is uniform, which can be explained by the effective viscosity, and the drag of the condensate layer on the water in the core of the hyphae. An estimation based on two possible observation scenarios suggests a Poiseuille flow profile needs a speed spread of 1.8--2.3, while the observed speed spread of the tipward stream is 1.23 (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_poiseuille_plug}). A flow profile close to plug flow is therefore a reasonable assumption, and the condensate moves nearly as a whole.
The condensate layer has a volumetric proportion $\theta$ of lipids and $(1-\theta)$ of water, and moves at a speed $v_m$.
In this model, we also imagine that the condensate layer is coating the walls, and that the water in the core of the hyphae is free to flow at a rate of $q_{w,r<a}$, with a radius $a$ smaller than the hyphal radius $b$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}B). In this model, we assume that all lipids are consumed at the tips, but it is easy to imagine that some lipids which are not part of $\theta$ are recirculated back to the root.

\end{equation}

Also add the explicit tree equation here, in the main text (agreed 11 Nov), to show that tree-like sections are trivial (q_w = 0, so q_c = q_s): v_b = v_m + (1-theta)/theta * J_l / (pi a^2). The network solve only matters in loops.
In the case of $q_w = 0$, the net water flow is zero, which corresponds to the case of a tree-like section of the network. In that case, the backflow carries the whole motor stream back through the core ($q_c = q_s$), and the rootward flow is opposite to the tipward flow and grows with $\Theta$, and $v_m$ alone, at fixed $\theta$.
It also suggests a possible explanation for the spatial and temporal variation of speeds.
% NOTE (11 Nov, meeting Coco, agreed: remove capital Theta): this Theta is the measured lipid fraction of the lumen (Bago 2002, SI fig theta_spatial); say it in words ('the lipid fraction of the lumen'), no symbol.
The spatial variation of $\Theta$~\cite{bago_tracking_2002} (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:theta_spatial}) indeed directly contributes to the magnitude of the core flow.

In looped sections of the network, $q_w$ is non-zero, and the backflow is routed into high-betweenness edges, making those higher in speed than low-betweenness edges (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}F).

Our conceptual model is supported by the data at the individual pipe level.
Cutting a segment out of the network brings the nearest tip closer, which in the model lowers the motor speed and with it the backflow, as observed after section cuts, where both streams slowed down a bit (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}C).
However, we wanted to know what its consequences would be at the network level.
Could it explain the observed variation of speed in space and time?
Because fluid movement happens at low Reynolds numbers in our system and the entrance length in each individual hyphal pipe is close to its diameter, the approximation of an infinite length cylinder we used to derive the flow profile in a single pipe is still justified in the network context (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_poiseuille_plug}).
A network of connected pipes is then analogous to a network of these individual circuit elements. The pressure at each node in the network is determined by the flow through each edge, and the flow through each edge is determined by the pressure difference between its two nodes. Flux is then analogous to current, and pressure difference to voltage.

Such an electrical circuit equivalent of the network can be solved with linear algebra using Kirchhoff's current law at each intersection, and requiring that the sum of pressure drops around loops in the network be zero (Methods). 
As in the case of growth-induced mass flow (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_mass_flow}), we set the sink magnitude at each node $k$ to correspond to newly created volume $\Delta V_k$ and added a global reservoir, connected to manually annotated nodes which are strongly suspected to be connected to roots.
Using lipid dry mass measurements, we can estimate the volume amount of lipid $J_l$ that needs to be transported to the tips to sustain growth, as a fixed share of $\Delta V_k$ (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_theta_choice}).
Additionally, we assume that the flows are routed to minimize the total power dissipation in the network.
This allows us to solve for the pressure at each node in the network, and therefore the demand $J_l$ through each edge. This is the first step of the solver, which is then used in the second step to calculate the backflow through each edge.

In each edge the lipid current fixes the size of the motor-driven condensate: with a lipid share $\theta$ of the condensate moving at the motor speed $v_m$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lipid_current}
J_l = \pi(b^2 - a^2)\theta v_m,
\end{equation}
so the core radius follows from $a^2 = b^2 - J_l/(\pi v_m \theta)$. An edge that carries more lipid has a thicker condensate and a narrower core. In order to limit the condensate to a physically reasonable size, we set a lower bound on the core radius $a \geq 0.8 b$ (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_theta_choice}).
% NOTE (11 Nov, meeting Coco, agreed: remove capital Theta): 'whole-lumen lipid fraction in the model, $\Theta$' -> say it in words, no symbol.
The growth data show that the condensate layer must be at least around $10\%$ lipid to supply the tips with growth-sustaining lipids without the core narrowing below its floor $a = 0.8\,b$. Literature reports total lipid fractions near the root~\cite{bago_translocation_2002, bago_tracking_2002} well above the corresponding whole-lumen lipid fraction in the model, $\Theta$, because $\theta$ is the lipid share of the thin condensate only. Following this lower bound, we take $\theta = 0.10$, a lower estimate among a wide possible range; the model's accuracy is flat over a wide range of $\theta$, so the exact value is partly arbitrary (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_theta_choice}).
The sign of $J_l$ determines the direction of motor flow.
Each edge then acts as a source of flux $q_s$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:motor_stream}) in parallel with the hydraulic conductance of its core (hyphal radius minus the condensate annulus), $K = \pi a^4/(8\mu L)$, so that its net water flux is $q_w = q_s - K\Delta p$ (Methods), where $\Delta p = p_j - p_i$ is the pressure difference along the edge from node $i$ to node $j$, the direction in which $q_s$ and $q_w$ are counted. 
These values are all derived from the previously solved $J_l$, and we use them in a second solver to find the backflow.
Solving these elements together for the pressures, with no water entering or leaving the network except at the roots, which are tied to a common reservoir (growth is neglected here, as it moves far less volume than the motors), gives $q_w$ on every edge, and with it the pressure-driven backflow through the core, $q_c = q_s - q_w$. 
From the calculated flow, we read out the backflow speed as $v_\mathrm{b} = |q_c|/(\pi a^2)$, the mean speed of the pressure-driven core flow relative to the moving condensate edge.
This is a read-out choice that reproduces the measured level of rootward speeds (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_poiseuille_plug}).
The model uses only measured or physical inputs besides the lipid share $\theta$ of the condensate: the network and its growth from the time-lapse, hyphal diameters from the network images recalibrated on the filmed hyphae, the measured tipward speed (Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}B), and $\theta = 0.10$ with $a \geq 0.8\,b$; no parameter is fitted to the rootward speeds.
In the model, $v_m$ is given by a calibrated function of the network distance $d$ to the nearest growing tip, fitted to the measured tipward speeds (Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}B; Methods).

To illustrate the model's characteristics, we first applied it to toy networks (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model} D-G). These were constructed with typical measured values of hyphal diameter, tipward speed and tip distance. A number of motifs regarding backflow speeds can be identified, which will be shown to be present in the real network as well.

Tree networks are the simple case where $q_w$ is zero, and the backflow is equal to the motor stream $q_s$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:motor_stream}) in the opposite direction (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}D).
Each outgoing branch from a junction carries the condensate demand of its downstream tips, which the lipid current $J_l$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:lipid_current}) has to match.
The more tips downstream, the more demand, and the thicker the condensate layer, which narrows the core (Eq.~\eqref{eq:lipid_current}) and increases the backflow speed, and the more asymmetric the flow profile is across the network. 

The increase in backflow speed with demand can be mitigated by a larger hyphal diameter, which increases the core radius and reduces the backflow speed at fixed demand. This is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}E, where a single path with a tapering diameter is shown. The backflow speed is higher in the narrower sections of the hypha, and lower in the wider sections.

Together, the increase of the motor speed $v_m$ with distance to tip (Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}B), and the increase in demand with the number of tips, create a rise in backflow speed with distance to tip (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}D,E).
This is somewhat mitigated by the tapering of hyphal diameters.

One feature this model also predicts is circulation in added loops, specifically if these have a conductance imbalance.
A length or diameter difference between two arms of a loop can create a circular flow: both arms share one pressure difference between their junctions, so the higher conductance arm carries more of the backflow than the lower conductance arm (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}F).
This results in the counter-intuitive situation where a wider arm of a loop can carry a faster backflow than the narrower arm, even though in the tree case the wider hypha would carry a slower backflow (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_tree_loop}).
This holds for both diameter and length differences, as the conductance of a hypha is proportional to its core radius $a$ to the fourth power and inversely proportional to its length.
In the case that the imbalance gets larger, the backflow in the lower conductance arm can in principle even reverse direction, and flow towards the tip instead of towards the root.
Finally, as tips multiply, they increase their demand for lipid, which increases the motor streaming and with it the backflow (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}G).

\section*{Discussion}

We present a series of measurements that map the flows across 5 different networks of AM fungi, and find structural relationships between their tipward and rootward flows.
Rootward flows along central edges display a variety of speeds, whereas tipward flows are steady around 3~$\mu$m/s.
We propose a model that explains this with a motor flow that delivers a lipid condensate to network tips.
The water carried with the condensate that growth does not use then returns as backflow through the rest of the network.
This backflow is pressure-driven, and will thus concentrate into central hyphae, where its speed can reach about twice the motor speed.

\subsection*{Measured flows and how far they can be trusted}
In this work, we have shown extensive analysis of flows in the hyphal network of AM fungi.
We show that in most hyphae, there are at least two opposing streams: a tipward stream narrowly around 3~$\mu$m/s, slower near the tips, and a rootward stream that is more variable and rises with distance from the tip (1,654 bright-field measurements in 15 sessions on 5 plates).
We also imaged lipid movement using NileRed staining, and found that the lipid moves in both flows, but is more likely to be found on the tipward stream, which is also more uniform in speed.

In trying to differentiate the mechanisms behind these two streams, we found that an osmotic shock changes only the rootward stream, which is therefore pressure-driven.
The tipward stream is insensitive to the shock. We interpret it as a lipid-containing condensate carried by molecular motors, but we did not test the motors directly; blocking microtubules or motors and checking whether the tipward stream stops, and the rootward stream with it, would be the decisive experiment.
After a root cut both streams continue, so neither needs the plant.
Isolated segments of a few millimeters keep both streams, slightly slower, so both are driven locally. 

For most of the analysis, we rely on bright-field imaging, where the 20 Hz frame rate allowed adequate sampling space to capture the flows.
Because the lid had to stay on during imaging to avoid flows reacting to air flow (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_lid}), a lot of optical resolution was lost between the hyphae and the objective.
This is exacerbated in fluorescence imaging, where the exposure requirements forced us to image most of those videos at 2 or 1 Hz.
This put limits on the speed range (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_forward_choices}; Methods, \nameref{subsec:fl_detection_limit}), so a thorough bright-field to fluorescence comparison had to be done on a subset of the videos.

We also have to be careful about extrapolating the model results to the entirety of the network graph, as there were biases in how the videos were made (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_video_selection}).
High edge-betweenness edges were favoured to be filmed as opposed to low edge-betweenness edges.
Finally, the hyphal diameter extraction, while effective in aggregate, is noisy and brought a lot of noise into the model.
Extracting hyphal diameter from the 2$\times$ network images requires us to guess at a diameter of a sub-optical resolution object under varying focus qualities (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_widths}).
While some aspects of the model were validated with the more accurate diameters extracted from videos, full network diameter data cannot be extracted in such a way, and therefore that limits the analysis. 

\subsection*{The model: what it explains and where it falls short}

With these data, we were able to present a model in which motors carry a lipid-containing condensate along the hyphal wall towards the tips, and the displaced fluid returns through the network as a pressure-driven flow.
Both the insensitivity to pressure of the motor-carried stream and the whole pressure field are important to capture the breadth of tipward and rootward measurements.
Key model characteristics like demand dependence (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}D), to a lesser extent diameter dependence (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}E), the ranking of the backflow between the two arms of a loop by the network solve (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig6_examples}B) and backflow increases due to growth (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}G) were captured in the measured data as well. 

Even our estimate of $\theta = 10\%$ lipid in the moving condensate, up to $3.6\%$ of the whole hyphal volume, suggests that a significant proportion of the volume of the hyphae is filled with lipids.
Such a proportion could easily lead to clogging if all of the lipids coalesce into a single phase.
What then is this large fraction of neutral lipid packaged within the cytoplasm?
The minimal unit of lipid packaging is generally thought to be membrane bound lipid droplets.
Such lipid droplets are generally kept from coalescing by coating~\cite{thiam_why_2017}.
In AM fungal hyphae, the lipid droplets keep a consistent diameter of about 1~$\mu$m, with no sign of coalescence~\cite{sister_lipid_droplets_inprep}.
The ensemble of those lipid droplets can then form an emulsion with a set lipid volume fraction.
The condensate, due to the repulsive strength between the different lipid droplets, can have different rheological properties than the rest of the cytoplasm~\cite{jeffrey_rheological_1976}.
We assume that this condensate is phase-separated from the bulk cytoplasm.

The perturbation experiments are also explained by the broader implications of the model.
An osmotic shock induces a sharp negative osmotic pressure at its application, causing a rush that dies down after a while.
The model has a root component, which merely serves as a large passive reservoir, replicating the idea that a root connection can take in the backflow without having to return it, but the severance of the root does not stop flows.
It does have implications for a rootless organism in the sense that the entire model bases itself on this tipward and rootward axis.
If there is no root, then on a large time-scale, the network could lose this orientation.
It has been shown AM fungal networks can still survive with myristate~\cite{kleinCytoplasmicFlowDynamics2026} in the medium, but the broader model implications are not explored here.
A series of cuts isolating a small segment also brings the flows down, because the tip is suddenly moved much closer to the point of measurement, leading to lower motor movement, leading to lower rootward movement.
This is assuming that tip slowdown is a combination of friction and bunching near a network terminus, and not connected to local anatomy that actively sets motor speed. 

In broader context, AM fungi trade with their plant hosts for carbon, and have been shown to have preference for favourable exchange rates~\cite{kiers_reciprocal_2011,bisot_carbon-phosphorous_2025}.
The model in this work as shown, could be a starting point to think of this preference not as an active process, but a result of physical processes in a flow network.
We can recreate the proportionality by thinking of P as being harvested by fungal tips as the network grows into the soil.
If this proportionality is to match the measured exchange rate of Bisot et al.~\cite{bisot_carbon-phosphorous_2025} (3--4 $\mu$g C per $\mu$g P by mass), it is important to note that the model predicts phosphorus in proportion to transported carbon, not to the carbon used for growth (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_cp_exchange}).
This distinction is testable.
The rootward stream is fast enough: at 3--4 $\mu$m/s it covers 1~cm in $\sim$45~min, consistent with $\geq$85\% of absorbed phosphorus reaching the root within $\sim$100~h~\cite{bisot_carbon-phosphorous_2025}.
One way to package the phosphate at such a high concentration is as polyphosphate, which has been shown to exist in AM fungi~\cite{hijikata_polyphosphate_2010,kikuchi_aquaporin-mediated_2016}.

We see that bidirectional resource movement and trade could be largely mechanically mediated.
Such a control mechanism has the advantage of being robust because the proportionality of flow is embedded in the physical properties of the system and, therefore, does not require additional biological control.
However, in our model, the path taken by the phosphorus in the network is strongly dependent on its relative cumulative hydraulic resistance and, therefore, on the edge radii.
Radial adaptation could constitute a flow control mechanism.
However, we never observed hyphal external radii diminishing.
Full control would probably require an additional ingredient of radial shrinkage. 

Where the model does fall short is its under-prediction on low edge-betweenness edges which are deep inside the network.
Some high edge-betweenness hyphae are also under-predicted, which we attribute to faults in the network extraction: because the pressure is solved over the whole network, a wrongly connected or missing hypha lets the solve pool the backflow in neighbouring edges rather than in the hypha that carries it (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_rerouting}).
Additionally, with enough of a conductance imbalance, the backflow can move in the same direction as the motor flows due to recirculation.
We have seen no clear case of this.

\subsection*{Relation to other systems and to the symbiosis}

Our results suggest that the lipids preferentially move in a direction informed by fungal growth.
Taking the steady $\sim$3~$\mu$m/s tipward stream as the motor-carried condensate, on loop edges, where the direction is not fixed by the tree structure, its direction matches the growth direction on 86\% of 677 edges.
It is likely that part of the network of transport filaments simply orients in the direction of tip growth at the moment of its creation and persists in that direction over longer timescales.
The tipward stream's insensitivity to pressure, its locality and its steady speed are consistent with the condensate holding this lipid being carried by molecular motors that move in one direction on polar cytoskeletal filaments.
This network of filaments inside the fungal network is mathematically equivalent to a weighted oriented graph, where the weight of a given edge can represent the number of cytoskeletal filaments, allowing molecular motor movement.
It is unclear how this oriented network organizes to maintain a coherent intensity and polarity with growth. 

Two network solutions to long-distance transport have been described in other eukaryotes: pressure-driven peristaltic streaming in \textit{Physarum}~\cite{alim_mechanism_2017} and motor transport on microtubules in the reticulate amoeba \textit{Filoreta ramosa}~\cite{guest_multiple_2026}.
In \textit{Filoreta}, nuclei, lysosomes and mitochondria move in both directions along microtubules at 5--8~$\mu$m/s, and their speed rises with branch diameter, which the authors link to wider branches holding more microtubules~\cite{guest_multiple_2026}.
Our tipward stream likewise rises with diameter (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_forward_flow}), whereas the rootward stream rises about twice as steeply.
AM fungi would thus combine both solutions, motors in one direction and pressure in the other.
The comparison has limits: \textit{Filoreta} is a wall-less amoeba without turgor, and it moves single organelles rather than a lipid condensate.

One way of thinking about the system is that it is mainly driven by supply.
Although in this paper our approach was to estimate what the flux of carbon was in each edge of the network from growth observation, it is possible that from the fungal point of view things work the other way around.
Growth would mainly emerge from the network's underlying molecular motor track organization.

This has the advantage of solving an informational bottleneck.
If a tip grows at one end of the network, how is the information about its carbon demand transmitted to the root-fungal interface where that carbon will eventually come from?
Instead of needing information transmission to match supply and demand and orient carbon fluxes accordingly, fungal tips could grow and branch according to the carbon supply of the existing network of molecular motor tracks.
Indeed, the net flux of lipid across the colony could be entirely determined by its orientation (track direction) and intensity (number of parallel tracks assuming tracks are saturated with motors) as well as by the plant supply (source).
The growth might then simply occur at a rate limited by the network-determined supply of lipids.
Where information does need to travel, the pressure-coupled backflow could offer a fast channel: a change in demand at one tip would alter the pressure across the connected network far faster than motors can carry cargo along the hyphae.

However, some abrupt topological changes, such as anastomosis or the colony abandoning growth in a specific region of space, could require a reorganization of the cytoskeletal filaments.
It is unclear how that reorganization is modulated in a way that allows one to maintain a flux of carbon that is consistent with the general fungal growth strategy.
Depending on the timing of the reorganization, it is also likely that the flux of lipids moving on microtubules will not always follow Kirchhoff's current law at some intersection.
Because lipids are incompressible and cannot accumulate in a node, this means that they will be recirculated in the internal cytoplasmic flow.
This phenomenon could explain the observation that lipids can also move in two directions in fungal hyphae.
In \textit{Filoreta}, Guest and Dawson infer antiparallel microtubule arrays where branches growing from opposite directions fused~\cite{guest_multiple_2026}, another lineage in which anastomosis would leave tracks pointing both ways in one branch.

In our model, we assumed that water could not flow out of the hyphae except at the hyphal-root interface.
However, AM fungi express functional aquaporins~\cite{li_first_2013,kikuchi_aquaporin-mediated_2016}, which could let water in and out along the hyphae.
Any water potential gradient between the inside and outside of the hyphae could then produce influx or outflux of water at any point of the network.
The resulting system would be analogous to the xylem-phloem system of plants.
In plants, the sugar loaded phloem generates an influx of water from the xylem.
This in turn generates a net flux of water through the phloem.
Any kind of osmolyte that would be produced inside the fungal network could very well drive a similar osmotic pump by changing the overall flow distribution in the network.
Kikuchi et al.\ found that suppressing host transpiration slowed the translocation of polyphosphate through AM hyphae towards the host, and proposed transpiration-driven hyphal water flow as its driver~\cite{kikuchi_aquaporin-mediated_2016}.
In our root-organ cultures there is no transpiration, yet both streams continue, also after the root is cut (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}B); the motor-driven circulation proposed here would act without a shoot, alongside transpiration where there is one.

Such effects may play a role in the transport of resources within the AM colony; they would add to the mechanism of bidirectional flow described here rather than replace it.
Regardless of the contribution of other mechanisms, our results indicate that lipid movement results in some fluid flow in the opposite direction.
Such backflows likely play a significant role in the full picture of AM fungal flows.
The framework developed here can also be flexibly expanded to include the effect of other kinds of carbon demand, such as spore growth or hyphal maintenance.
By adding sources and sinks of water where aquaporins exchange it with the surroundings, it is also possible to account for additional transport mechanisms.

\section*{Conclusion}
In short, we have shown that within AM fungi, a minimal model in which motors carry a lipid-containing condensate towards the tips and a pressure-driven backflow returns cytoplasm to the root recreates the range and dynamics of measured flows. Its limitations are in the noise of the measured parameters. One consequence of this model is its physical result of carbon-phosphorus exchange, which in the model can be described as a linear relationship. In the future, phosphorus packing and the role of water in AM fungal transport should be investigated to see further effects. Microtubule dynamics in AM fungi are also an open field to investigate, as they have not been widely imaged in vivo before.
