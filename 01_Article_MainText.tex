\begin{abstract}
 
The symbiosis between plants and arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi is fundamental to terrestrial ecosystems driving large flows of soil nutrients and carbon on a planetary scale. 
Yet, the mechanism underlying the transport of those resources within their microscopic hyphae has not been entirely elucidated. We imaged transport dynamics in high resolution at multiple locations in growing and perturbed networks. A large part of the carbon flowed steadily toward the growing tips, whereas cytoplasmic flow in the other direction varied widely across the network. Consistent with these observations, we propose a model of network wide bidirectional flow in which a lipid rich phase driven by molecular motors builds pressure that generates cytoplasmic backflow. The model recapitulates much of the variations of backflow speed offering insights into how a network spreading over centimeters as an open-pipe network can coordinate complex flow patterns. The tight coupling between carbon transport and cytoplasmic backflow may provide a simple, robust explanation for the 400-million-year-old reciprocity of resource exchange between plants and AM fungi.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

Symbiotic nutrient exchange between arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi and their host plants is fundamental to global biogeochemical cycles. It is estimated that \textcolor{blue}{XXGt} of carbon flow from plant to their partner every year, allowing the growth of the fungal networks in the soil. These networks acquire nutrients that are transported back to the host plant. Yet the mechanism driving the movement of both nutrients and carbon within the fungal network remains unknown. 

Nutrients and carbon do not only move but also need to be directed to where they are needed. Generally, carbon, in the form of lipids, must be continuously transported away from the plant toward the growing fungal tips where it is consumed, while nutrients such as phosphorus must be transported back in the opposite direction. Our group previously estimated that, when a single AM fungus was attached to a single plant, the fungal use of carbon for growth was proportional to the flux of phosphorus toward the plant~\cite{bisot_carbon-phosphorous_2025}.

However, the flow of nutrients and carbon is not simply a matter of transport in two opposing directions: a single fungal network can interact with multiple plant hosts~\cite{kiers_reciprocal_2011,walder_mycorrhizal_2012}.
Without selective allocation of nutrients to partners that engage in a proportional effort to provide carbon, ``free ride'' plant phenotypes can emerge that reap the benefits of symbiosis while avoiding costs~\cite{kiers_reciprocal_2011,bever_preferential_2009,walder_regulation_2015,noe_mycorrhizal_2018,kiers_misconceptions_2016}. Indeed, it has been observed that, when a single AM network was connected to two different plant hosts that differed in their carbon supply, more phosphorus was directed toward the plant that provided more carbon~\cite{kiers_reciprocal_2011}. 

Past research has quantified the flow speed in both directions in networks of different ages~\cite{oyarte_galvez_travelling-wave_2025} suggesting that flows could be actively modulated. AM fungi have aseptate hyphae, meaning that the organism can be considered a hydraulically connected system of open pipes. What mechanisms explain the network-wide bidirectional transport of resources and its modulation over space and time in a way that is coherent with its growth and ecology?

To answer that question, we acquired image data at two different spatial and temporal resolutions to quantify flow in specific edges of growing networks.
At high spatial and temporal resolution, we acquired bright-field videos of transport dynamics within single hyphal edges over multiple days in perturbed and unperturbed networks.
Using NileRed, a neutral lipid-specific fluorescent dye, we also characterized and quantified the movement of carbon in the form of lipids along these same edges.
At low spatial and temporal resolution, we obtained time-lapse images of growing networks that were transformed into their equivalent mathematical graph.
In this way, we can relate the growth of the network with the measured flow speeds observed in the same network, allowing us to explain high-resolution observations both mechanistically and quantitatively.
From these data and findings, we construct a minimal model that explains the observed bidirectional transport of resources in the colony and its modulation over space and time.

\section*{Results}
\namedsubsection{Asymmetric modulation of bidirectional speeds}{subsec:res_speeds}

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal networks (\textit{Rhizophagus irregularis}) grow through the extension of fungal tips away from the root of the host plant (\textit{Daucus carota}, Methods, \nameref{subsec:strains}, \cite{oyarte_galvez_travelling-wave_2025}).
Over time, these elongated hyphae develop into a branched mycelial structure where anastomoses (fusions between a hyphal tip and a hyphal segment) create loops behind the growing front ~\cite{oyarte_galvez_travelling-wave_2025}.
The network's topological context therefore varies along the length of a hypha. Close to the tip, the network is very much tree-like: about 45\% of the filmed edges within 1~mm of a tip hang from a single hypha, with no loop and no root of their own beyond them. This means that, as we move away from a growing tip across branching point, the number of downstream growing tip that depend on a given edge increases; about 10\% of the edges further than 2~mm from a tip are tree-like. (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}A; SI Fig.~\ref{fig:tree_like_share}).

We asked how this change in topological context affected the speeds of the flows in the two directions along a hypha: toward the growing tip (tipward) and toward the root (rootward), as defined by the growth direction of the network.
We imaged flows at high resolution in 731 bright-field videos along the hyphae of five colonies (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:video_positions}) and, mostly at the same positions, in 625 videos in fluorescence where neutral lipids were stained with NileRed. For the example network of Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}A--C,F, the videos were taken over three consecutive days.
We followed mainly runner hyphae, the main routes to the root that contained the edges of high betweenness centrality~\cite{kirkley_betweenness_2018}. In those hyphae, the tipward and rootward flows could be distinguished by eye (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_video_selection}; SI Fig.~\ref{fig:bc_selection}). 

In undisturbed hyphae (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_lid}), the two flows are superimposed in the video. Particles move in straight lines at nearly constant speed. In a kymograph, a 2d spatiotemporal projection of the video, every moving particle leaves a straight diagonal streak, with no sign of random or back-and-forth movement, except near the tips (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}D; SI Fig.~\ref{fig:kymograph_examples}).
In the field of view, the directed movement exceeds diffusion by one to two orders of magnitude, ruling out diffusion as a movement mechanism, and pointing to directed transport, so the slope of a streak is its speed (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_peclet}).
Because each detected streak corresponded to one speed, we could reduce each kymograph to  distributions of tipward and rootward speeds (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}D,E, SI Fig.~\ref{fig:speed_flux_extraction_real_data}).
The distribution of tipward speed was generally narrowly peaked around a median value close to 3~$\mu$m/s (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}E,F, SI Fig.~\ref{fig:speed_iqr}).

Rootward speeds were more broadly distributed and their median value generally increased with distance from the tip (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}F).
We looked at the distribution of median speed across all videos acquired across 15 imaging days from 5 different growing networks. We observed that the distribution of median tipward speeds was also narrowly peaked around 3~$\mu$m/s and rarely exceeded 4~$\mu$m/s while the distribution of rootward speed was wider with a large portions of speeds above 4~$\mu$m/sFig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries} A. In about half of the edges, the rootward speed was faster than the tipward speed and the rootward speed was only slower in a small portion of the edges Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}A.
A similar dependence on the distance to the nearest growing tip was observed on the whole dataset of growing tips from all positions across networks. Tipward speed increased only slightly in the first two millimeters and had an otherwise flat and narrow profile. Rootward speeds tended to increase by two fold in the first 5 mm away from a growing tip and then plateaued but the dependence on the nearest growing tip was less tight.(Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}B).

Because the statistics of rootward and tipward speeds differed strongly, we hypothesized that transport in each direction involved different cellular components and was driven by distinct mechanisms. Since we knew carbon used for network growth could only come from the plant root and needed to be transported across the network, we hypothesized that the tipward movement could in part be composed of lipids, the main form of carbon that the plant provides to the fungus~\cite{luginbuehl_fatty_2017}. 
We imaged these lipids with NileRed fluorescent dye~\cite{bago_tracking_2002} and compared the fluorescence videos to bright-field videos of the same positions. At the resolution of the fluorescence videos (0.14~$\mu$m per pixel, 0.5 or 1~s per frame, 10 to 20~s per video), we saw no evidence of lipid particles randomly switching direction, as they would in the random walk underlying diffusive motion (Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}C). (Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}C). In some cases, where the bright-field video shows a balanced set of tipward and rootward streaks, imaging the stained lipids revealed a larger proportion of tipward moving streaks. 
To quantify how general this difference was, we calculated the direction bias of detected streaks, the direction bias of detected streaks in all edges (Methods, \nameref{subsec:direction_bias}).We found that while bright-field videos generally showed a balanced distribution of tipward and rootward flows, in a large proportion of the edges (xx\%) the lipid movement was significantly biased tipward.(Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}D).

The difference in flow behavior in tipward and rootward directions suggests that each flow. Mechanical pressure gradients has been suggested in the past as a potential driver of flow ~\cite{heaton_growth-induced_2010,lew_mass_2005,lew_how_2011}. While it cannot explain the simultaneous presence of flows in two directions in the same hypha (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_mass_flow}), it is possible that such mechanism could drive flow in one direction. 
An ideal experiment to test this is to modify the pressure gradient across the network and see how it affects flows~\cite{lew_mass_2005}. This can be done by creating an extracellular osmotic gradient~\cite{lew_mass_2005}.
Following a similar approach, we added a drop of dissolved NaCl solution (200 mOsm) (Methods, \nameref{subsec:perturbations}) to the base of the network a few centimeters behind the growing front and imaged near the growth edge (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}A).
We expected that, increasing extracellular osmotic pressure would locally pull water out of the hyphae reducing the turgor pressure within the affected edges. Such non-uniform change in turgor-pressure would change the pressure gradient across the network and change those flows that are pressure driven..
We observed that the flow speed in the tip direction was almost unaffected by the addition of the salt solution (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}A); fluorescence imaging of the lipids showed that their movement in the tip direction was also unaffected (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:salt_shock}D).
By contrast, the flow in the root direction were transiently sped up by the addition of the drop. 

This experiment suggested that rootward flows were likely pressure driven. We asked how pressure gradient could build up that would drive flows in the root direction. Past work proposed that plant host transpiration could be the driving force explaining the flow of resources toward the root~\cite{kikuchi_aquaporin-mediated_2016}.
Although the plant in our root-organ culture does not produce leaves and, therefore, does not evapotranspirate, we tested whether the connection to the plant was necessary for the flows in the two directions to be maintained.
In one network, we imaged a runner hypha at multiple positions from root to tip to measure the velocity profile along its length.
We then severed the network from the root with a scalpel by cutting across the whole bottom half of the fungal comparment (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}B). We then recorded videos from the same positions in the first half hour after the cut and then again in the second half hour.
We observed that flows continued in both directions at all time.
About 20~min after the cut the rootward stream had slowed down slightly, while the tipward speed stayed at about 3~$\mu$m/s (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}B).
By 50~min the rootward stream had recovered near the tip (SI, \nameref{sec:si_perturbations}). A similar profile in each direction to what had been observed in unperturbed networks remained throughout (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}B, Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}A) suggesting that the plant root was neither the driving force nor an important element modulating flows on such timescales.
This matches earlier work showing that flows can still be active for a year without a plant host~\cite{kleinCytoplasmicFlowDynamics2026}and rules out transpiration by the plant as their driver.

Because the network is hydraulically connected, any of its node could in theory be a pressure sink or source driving flows throughout. To find if only a subset of nodes was contributing to the flows, we made targeted cuts to isolate small segments from the network and image flows at sufficient distance from the cut-induced stress (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}C).
In patches of network as small as only a few millimeters in total length, we still observed flows in the two directions (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}C).
We observed that the flow speed in both directions was slowed down a bit, but the flow never stopped (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}C).
These experiments demonstrate that neither of the flows in any of the two directions requires long range connectivity of the network to operate (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_segment_size}). 

\namedsubsection{Flow model}{subsec:res_hypothesis}

What transport mechanisms would be compatible with all those observations? For tipward flows, such mechanisms should be insensitive to perturbation of pressure gradients and display a largely constant speed around $3\mu m/s$ (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_forward_flow}).
We hypothesized that tipward flow could be driven by cargo carried by molecular motors along microtubules. Those are indeed present throughout AM fungal hyphae and oriented in longitudinal filaments and bundles ~\cite{timonen_microtubules_2001}. The speed of the tipward flows ($\sim3\mu m/s$) is consistent with the range of speeds of molecular motors~\cite{steinberg_cell_2017}.
Lipid droplets are believed to be transported by molecular motors in AM fungi~\cite{bago_translocation_2002}, and other fungal species have been shown to transport lipids along microtubules~\cite{guimaraes_peroxisomes_2015, lin_active_2016}.
Other evidence comes from the imaging of lipids in confocal microscopy that reveal condensate of small lipid droplets that follow visible tracks within hyphae~\cite{sister_lipid_droplets_inprep}.
Flows toward the root are sensitive to pressure changes from osmotic shocks (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}A).
The flows toward the root are also more variable in speed.
This suggests that the mechanism driving these flows is different from the mechanism driving the tipward flows and is likely pressure driven.

We were also struck by the fact that such pressure driven flow would continue even in small isolated network segments. This indicated that pressure gradient could build up in small segments, independent of long range connectivity of the network. This led us to hypothesize that such pressure build up could in fact be driven by the tipward movement of a lipid-containing condensate.
We wanted to know if integrating such a simple idea into a minimal model of network flow could explain the observed bidirectional transport of resources and its modulation over space and time.

Every edge is considered to contain a motor-driven flow $q_m$, and a pressure-driven flow $q_p$. Each edge is considered polarized, such that it has a direction where motors point. In this model, they point towards tips (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}A). The motors then move a lipid-containing condensate at their motor speed $v_m(d)$, where $d$ is the distance to the nearest tip. This function is fitted onto the measured tipward speed data (Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}B; Methods).

While there is evidence the motor flows occur along the walls of the hyphae (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_wall_motors}), we consider both flows to travel through arbitrary cross-section geometries, at a uniform speed profile. For pressure flows, this is consistent with the measured speed spreads, when comparing between hypothetical plug and Poiseuille profiles (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_poiseuille_plug}). For a given hyphal cross-section with area $A = \pi r^2$, a fraction $f_m$ is reserved for motor flow, and a fraction $1-f_m$ is reserved for pressure-driven flows (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}B).

Motor flows carry lipids in the form of droplets towards the tips, where they all get consumed for growth. The lipid flux at each edge is written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lipid_current}
J_{ij} = \theta f_m A\, v_m,
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is the concentration of lipid droplets within the motor flow. In the full model, we will calculate the lipid flux at each edge, and combined with the set motor flow fraction, the local concentration can be calculated. The non-lipid fraction $(1-\theta)$ is the volume that then returns in the backflow. While the model assumes that this is just water, we can imagine that it also contains unconsumed lipids.

The buildup of volume from the motor flows causes a pressure gradient which forces the pressure-based flows to move.

Zooming out, a single edge can be said to carry the following balance of non-lipid flows:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:net_inner_flow}
q_w = q_m - q_p,
\end{equation}
where $q_w$ is the net water flow. Motor flow can be written as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:motor_stream}
q_m = (1-\theta)\, f_m A\, v_m,
\end{equation}
and pressure-driven flows are stated as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:core_flux}
q_p = K \Delta p, \qquad K = \frac{(1-f_m)^2 A^2}{8 \pi \mu L},
\end{equation}
which is the Poiseuille conductance of a circle with the same area $(1-f_m)A$. In the case where edges are part of a tree-like section of the network, it follows that the net flux is zero ($q_w = 0$), meaning that $q_p = q_m$.

With these flows set up, we can construct an electrical circuit equivalent of the network, where each edge acts as a linear element, because at low Reynolds number each flow is proportional to its pressure drop. The first step in this setup is to find out where the motors are pointing, and what the local lipid concentration is in the motor flows. This is done by measuring the growth $dV/dt$ of each tip in a given network (Methods). This growth can be converted into a sink term for lipid flux (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_theta_choice}), which has to have been supplied by the root. We use minimum dissipation as a model for how the lipids are then routed from the base nodes in the network (where the fungal network connects to the host roots, annotated by hand in Methods, all tied to one common reservoir) to the tip nodes, yielding a $J_{ij}$ at each edge. The sign of $J_{ij}$ is used to orient the motor flow, and its magnitude is used to calculate the local lipid concentration $\theta$.

The motor directions, motor flow fraction $f_m$, the concentration and the fitted motor speed $v_m$ are all used then to calculate motor flow $q_m$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:motor_stream}). These are source terms in parallel with a resistor with conductance $K$. The hyphae are filled with incompressible cytoplasm, so water can only enter or leave the network at the base nodes. Growth is left out of this step, as it moves far less volume than the motor flow (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_volume_budget}). We use Kirchhoff's laws to solve for the pressures at each node, giving us the $\Delta p_{ij}$ necessary to calculate $q_p$ for every edge.

Once flows have been solved, the question is what these will look like under a microscope. To compare, we need to convert these to speeds. For this, we assume plug flows, as above, and therefore the backflow speed is calculated
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:backflow_speed}
v_\mathrm{b} = \frac{|q_p|}{(1-f_m) A}.
\end{equation}
This follows from the speed spreads, which are flatter than a Poiseuille profile and consistent with a noisy plug (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_poiseuille_plug}).

To illustrate the model's characteristics, we apply it to toy networks (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}D-G). These were constructed with typical measured values of hyphal diameter, tipward speed and tip distance. A number of motifs regarding backflow speeds can be identified. Tree networks, where $q_w = 0$, follow the motor speed alone, so the backflow speed rises with distance from the tip (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}D):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:tree_backflow_speed}
v_\mathrm{b}\big|_{q_w = 0} = (1-\theta)\frac{f_m}{1-f_m}\, v_m.
\end{equation}
Counterintuitively, hyphal diameter has no impact on the speeds in these tree-like sections (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}E), as the ratio between motor and pressure flows stays the same. Hyphal diameter does matter once loops are introduced. Due to conductance differences, either from length or diameter, flows are routed preferentially into higher conductance edges (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}F). The model also responds to temporal changes, showing that a growing model will have a profile that travels with its tip location (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}G).

Our model uses an extracted network from two snapshots taken at most 12~h apart to determine growth. Topology, geometry and hyphal diameter are all directly measured from the snapshots as well. Motor speeds follow the measured tipward profile (Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}B). The motor share of the cross-section, $f_m = 0.53$, is calibrated on the rootward speeds of tree-like network sections (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_theta_choice}). The lipid concentration $\theta$ follows from the growth demand, so no lipid constant is set. Nothing is fitted to optimize all measured rootward speeds.

Our conceptual model is supported by the data at the individual pipe level.
Cutting a segment out of the network brings the nearest tip closer, which in the model lowers the motor speed and with it the backflow, as observed after section cuts, where both streams slowed down a bit (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}C).

\section*{Discussion}

We present a series of measurements that map the flows across 5 different networks of AM fungi, and find structural relationships between their tipward and rootward flows.
Rootward flows along central edges display a variety of speeds, whereas tipward flows are steady around 3~$\mu$m/s.
We propose a model that explains this with a motor flow that delivers a lipid condensate to network tips.
The water carried with the condensate that growth does not use then returns as backflow through the rest of the network.
This backflow is pressure-driven, and will thus concentrate into central hyphae, where its speed can reach about twice the motor speed.

\subsection*{Measured flows and how far they can be trusted}
In this work, we have shown extensive analysis of flows in the hyphal network of AM fungi.
We show that in most hyphae, there are at least two opposing streams: a tipward stream narrowly around 3~$\mu$m/s, slower near the tips, and a rootward stream that is more variable and rises with distance from the tip (1,654 bright-field measurements over 15 imaging days on 5 plates).
We also imaged lipid movement using NileRed staining, and found that the lipid moves in both flows, but is more likely to be found on the tipward stream, which is also more uniform in speed.

In trying to differentiate the mechanisms behind these two streams, we found that an osmotic shock changes only the rootward stream, which is therefore pressure-driven.
The tipward stream is insensitive to the shock. We interpret it as a lipid-containing condensate carried by molecular motors, but we did not test the motors directly; blocking microtubules or motors and checking whether the tipward stream stops, and the rootward stream with it, would be the decisive experiment.
After a root cut both streams continue, so neither needs the plant.
Isolated segments of a few millimeters keep both streams, slightly slower, so both are driven locally. 

For most of the analysis, we rely on bright-field imaging, where the 20 Hz frame rate allowed adequate sampling space to capture the flows.
Because the lid had to stay on during imaging to avoid flows reacting to air flow (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_lid}), a lot of optical resolution was lost between the hyphae and the objective.
This is exacerbated in fluorescence imaging, where the exposure requirements forced us to image most of those videos at 2 or 1 Hz.
This put limits on the speed range (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_forward_choices}; Methods, \nameref{subsec:fl_detection_limit}), so a thorough bright-field to fluorescence comparison had to be done on a subset of the videos.

We also have to be careful about extrapolating the model results to the entirety of the network graph, as there were biases in how the videos were made (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_video_selection}).
High edge-betweenness edges were favored to be filmed as opposed to low edge-betweenness edges.
Finally, the hyphal diameter extraction, while effective in aggregate, is noisy and brought a lot of noise into the model.
Extracting hyphal diameter from the 2$\times$ network images requires us to guess at a diameter of a sub-optical resolution object under varying focus qualities (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_widths}).
While some aspects of the model were validated with the more accurate diameters extracted from videos, full network diameter data cannot be extracted in such a way, and therefore that limits the analysis. 

\subsection*{The model: what it explains and where it falls short}

% (1) Old sentences: "It also suggests a possible explanation for the spatial and temporal variation of speeds. % (2) Betweenness: loops route the backflow onto high-betweenness edges (old text: "In looped sections of the network, q_w is non-zero, and the backflow is routed into high-betweenness edges, making those higher in speed than low-betweenness edges (Fig 4F)"). For discussion.
% (3) Unconsumed lipid: the backflow may carry some lipid that was not consumed at the tips; could explain lipid seen moving rootward in NileRed (Fig 2D) and ties to the C:P trade paragraph.

With these data, we were able to present a model in which motors carry a lipid-containing condensate along the hyphal wall toward the tips, and the displaced fluid returns through the network as a pressure-driven flow.
Both the insensitivity to pressure of the motor-carried stream and the whole pressure field are important to capture the breadth of tipward and rootward measurements.
Key model characteristics like demand dependence (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}D), to a lesser extent diameter dependence (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}E), the ranking of the backflow between the two arms of a loop by the network solve (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig6_examples}B) and backflow increases due to growth (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}G) were captured in the measured data as well. 

Even our estimate of $\theta = 10\%$ lipid in the moving condensate, up to $3.6\%$ of the whole hyphal volume, suggests that a significant proportion of the volume of the hyphae is filled with lipids.
Such a proportion could easily lead to clogging if all of the lipids coalesce into a single phase.
What then is this large fraction of neutral lipid packaged within the cytoplasm?
The minimal unit of lipid packaging is generally thought to be membrane bound lipid droplets.
Such lipid droplets are generally kept from coalescing by coating~\cite{thiam_why_2017}.
In AM fungal hyphae, the lipid droplets keep a consistent diameter of about 1~$\mu$m, with no sign of coalescence~\cite{sister_lipid_droplets_inprep}.
The ensemble of those lipid droplets can then form an emulsion with a set lipid volume fraction.
The condensate, due to the repulsive strength between the different lipid droplets, can have different rheological properties than the rest of the cytoplasm~\cite{jeffrey_rheological_1976}.
We assume that this condensate is phase-separated from the bulk cytoplasm.

The perturbation experiments are also explained by the broader implications of the model.
An osmotic shock induces a sharp negative osmotic pressure at its application, causing a rush that dies down after a while.
The model has a root component, which merely serves as a large passive reservoir, replicating the idea that a root connection can take in the backflow without having to return it, but the severance of the root does not stop flows.
It does have implications for a rootless organism in the sense that the entire model bases itself on this tipward and rootward axis.
If there is no root, then on a large time-scale, the network could lose this orientation.
It has been shown AM fungal networks can still survive with myristate~\cite{kleinCytoplasmicFlowDynamics2026} in the medium, but the broader model implications are not explored here.
A series of cuts isolating a small segment also brings the flows down, because the tip is suddenly moved much closer to the point of measurement, leading to lower motor movement, leading to lower rootward movement.
This is assuming that tip slowdown is a combination of friction and bunching near a network terminus, and not connected to local anatomy that actively sets motor speed. 

In broader context, AM fungi trade with their plant hosts for carbon, and have been shown to have preference for favorable exchange rates~\cite{kiers_reciprocal_2011,bisot_carbon-phosphorous_2025}.
The model in this work as shown, could be a starting point to think of this preference not as an active process, but a result of physical processes in a flow network.
We can recreate the proportionality by thinking of P as being harvested by fungal tips as the network grows into the soil.
If this proportionality is to match the measured exchange rate of Bisot et al.~\cite{bisot_carbon-phosphorous_2025} (3--4 $\mu$g C per $\mu$g P by mass), it is important to note that the model predicts phosphorus in proportion to transported carbon, not to the carbon used for growth (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_cp_exchange}).
This distinction is testable.
The rootward stream is fast enough: at 3--4 $\mu$m/s it covers 1~cm in $\sim$45~min, consistent with $\geq$85\% of absorbed phosphorus reaching the root within $\sim$100~h~\cite{bisot_carbon-phosphorous_2025}.
One way to package the phosphate at such a high concentration is as polyphosphate, which has been shown to exist in AM fungi~\cite{hijikata_polyphosphate_2010,kikuchi_aquaporin-mediated_2016}.

We see that bidirectional resource movement and trade could be largely mechanically mediated.
Such a control mechanism has the advantage of being robust because the proportionality of flow is embedded in the physical properties of the system and, therefore, does not require additional biological control.
However, in our model, the path taken by the phosphorus in the network is strongly dependent on its relative cumulative hydraulic resistance and, therefore, on the edge radii.
Radial adaptation could constitute a flow control mechanism.
However, we never observed hyphal external radii diminishing.
Full control would probably require an additional ingredient of radial shrinkage. 

Where the model does fall short is its under-prediction on low edge-betweenness edges which are deep inside the network.
Some high edge-betweenness hyphae are also under-predicted, which we attribute to faults in the network extraction: because the pressure is solved over the whole network, a wrongly connected or missing hypha lets the solve pool the backflow in neighboring edges rather than in the hypha that carries it (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_rerouting}).
Additionally, with enough of a conductance imbalance, the backflow can move in the same direction as the motor flows due to recirculation.
We have seen no clear case of this.

\subsection*{Relation to other systems and to the symbiosis}

Our results suggest that the lipids preferentially move in a direction informed by fungal growth.
Taking the steady $\sim$3~$\mu$m/s tipward stream as the motor-carried condensate, on loop edges, where the direction is not fixed by the tree structure, its direction matches the growth direction on 86\% of 677 edges.
It is likely that part of the network of transport filaments simply orients in the direction of tip growth at the moment of its creation and persists in that direction over longer timescales.
The tipward stream's insensitivity to pressure, its locality and its steady speed are consistent with the condensate holding this lipid being carried by molecular motors that move in one direction on polar cytoskeletal filaments.
This network of filaments inside the fungal network is mathematically equivalent to a weighted oriented graph, where the weight of a given edge can represent the number of cytoskeletal filaments, allowing molecular motor movement.
It is unclear how this oriented network organizes to maintain a coherent intensity and polarity with growth. 

Two network solutions to long-distance transport have been described in other eukaryotes: pressure-driven peristaltic streaming in \textit{Physarum}~\cite{alim_mechanism_2017} and motor transport on microtubules in the reticulate amoeba \textit{Filoreta ramosa}~\cite{guest_multiple_2026}.
In \textit{Filoreta}, nuclei, lysosomes and mitochondria move in both directions along microtubules at 5--8~$\mu$m/s, and their speed rises with branch diameter, which the authors link to wider branches holding more microtubules~\cite{guest_multiple_2026}.
Our tipward stream likewise rises with diameter (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_forward_flow}), whereas the rootward stream rises about twice as steeply.
AM fungi would thus combine both solutions, motors in one direction and pressure in the other.
The comparison has limits: \textit{Filoreta} is a wall-less amoeba without turgor, and it moves single organelles rather than a lipid condensate.

One way of thinking about the system is that it is mainly driven by supply.
Although in this paper our approach was to estimate what the flux of carbon was in each edge of the network from growth observation, it is possible that from the fungal point of view things work the other way around.
Growth would mainly emerge from the network's underlying molecular motor track organization.

This has the advantage of solving an informational bottleneck.
If a tip grows at one end of the network, how is the information about its carbon demand transmitted to the root-fungal interface where that carbon will eventually come from?
Instead of needing information transmission to match supply and demand and orient carbon fluxes accordingly, fungal tips could grow and branch according to the carbon supply of the existing network of molecular motor tracks.
Indeed, the net flux of lipid across the colony could be entirely determined by its orientation (track direction) and intensity (number of parallel tracks assuming tracks are saturated with motors) as well as by the plant supply (source).
The growth might then simply occur at a rate limited by the network-determined supply of lipids.
Where information does need to travel, the pressure-coupled backflow could offer a fast channel: a change in demand at one tip would alter the pressure across the connected network far faster than motors can carry cargo along the hyphae.

However, some abrupt topological changes, such as anastomosis or the colony abandoning growth in a specific region of space, could require a reorganization of the cytoskeletal filaments.
It is unclear how that reorganization is modulated in a way that allows one to maintain a flux of carbon that is consistent with the general fungal growth strategy.
Depending on the timing of the reorganization, it is also likely that the flux of lipids moving on microtubules will not always follow Kirchhoff's current law at some intersection.
Because lipids are incompressible and cannot accumulate in a node, this means that they will be recirculated in the internal cytoplasmic flow.
This phenomenon could explain the observation that lipids can also move in two directions in fungal hyphae.
In \textit{Filoreta}, Guest and Dawson infer antiparallel microtubule arrays where branches growing from opposite directions fused~\cite{guest_multiple_2026}, another lineage in which anastomosis would leave tracks pointing both ways in one branch.

In our model, we assumed that water could not flow out of the hyphae except at the hyphal-root interface.
However, AM fungi express functional aquaporins~\cite{li_first_2013,kikuchi_aquaporin-mediated_2016}, which could let water in and out along the hyphae.
Any water potential gradient between the inside and outside of the hyphae could then produce influx or outflux of water at any point of the network.
The resulting system would be analogous to the xylem-phloem system of plants.
In plants, the sugar loaded phloem generates an influx of water from the xylem.
This in turn generates a net flux of water through the phloem.
Any kind of osmolyte that would be produced inside the fungal network could very well drive a similar osmotic pump by changing the overall flow distribution in the network.
Kikuchi et al.\ found that suppressing host transpiration slowed the translocation of polyphosphate through AM hyphae toward the host, and proposed transpiration-driven hyphal water flow as its driver~\cite{kikuchi_aquaporin-mediated_2016}.
In our root-organ cultures there is no transpiration, yet both streams continue, also after the root is cut (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}B); the motor-driven circulation proposed here would act without a shoot, alongside transpiration where there is one.

Such effects may play a role in the transport of resources within the AM colony; they would add to the mechanism of bidirectional flow described here rather than replace it.
Regardless of the contribution of other mechanisms, our results indicate that lipid movement results in some fluid flow in the opposite direction.
Such backflows likely play a significant role in the full picture of AM fungal flows.
The framework developed here can also be flexibly expanded to include the effect of other kinds of carbon demand, such as spore growth or hyphal maintenance.
By adding sources and sinks of water where aquaporins exchange it with the surroundings, it is also possible to account for additional transport mechanisms.

\section*{Conclusion}
In short, we have shown that within AM fungi, a minimal model in which motors carry a lipid-containing condensate toward the tips and a pressure-driven backflow returns cytoplasm to the root recreates the range and dynamics of measured flows. Its limitations are in the noise of the measured parameters. One consequence of this model is its physical result of carbon-phosphorus exchange, which in the model can be described as a linear relationship. In the future, phosphorus packing and the role of water in AM fungal transport should be investigated to see further effects. Microtubule dynamics in AM fungi are also an open field to investigate, as they have not been widely imaged in vivo before.
