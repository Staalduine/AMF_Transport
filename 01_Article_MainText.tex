\begin{abstract}
 
 The symbiosis between plants and arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi is fundamental to terrestrial ecosystems as it is responsible for large flows of nutrients on a planetary scale.
In this symbiosis, two distinct organisms trade phosphorus (P) for energy in the form of carbon (C).
However, the mechanism behind the bidirectional transport of resources within their microscopic hyphae has not been entirely elucidated.
We imaged transport dynamics of nutrient flows in high resolution in different positions in growing networks, and also perturbed flows through section cuts and osmotic shocks.
Our data suggest that carbon, as lipid, travels towards the growing tips within a condensate that molecular motors carry in one direction.
Our findings also suggest a tight coupling between network growth, internal carbon (lipid) transport dynamics, and bidirectional fluid movement within the network.
In an open pipe system, moving this condensate towards the tips pushes cytoplasmic fluid back towards the root, a backflow that may carry phosphorus.
Our model provides an intuitive understanding for the observed proportionality in resource exchange between the two organisms.

Also, the perturbations listed are segment cuts and osmotic shocks, and the root cut is missing.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}

The symbiosis between arbuscular mycorrhizal (AM) fungi and their host plants has existed for 400 million years~\cite{remy_four_1994,brundrett_evolutionary_2018}.
For the symbiosis to endure this long, robust mechanisms must be in place to ensure its stability.
Indeed, symbiosis can be a many to many relationship, which means that the plant can interact with multiple independent fungal colonies and one fungal network can interact with multiple plant hosts~\cite{kiers_reciprocal_2011,walder_mycorrhizal_2012}.
Without selective orientation of nutrients towards partners that engage in a proportional effort to provide nutrients, "free ride" plant or fungal phenotypes can emerge that reap the benefits of symbiosis while avoiding costs~\cite{kiers_reciprocal_2011,bever_preferential_2009,walder_regulation_2015,noe_mycorrhizal_2018,kiers_misconceptions_2016}. 

The fungal partner forms a network in the soil using plant derived carbon.
It absorbs phosphorus from the soil, which is then transported back to the plant.
This means that carbon must constantly be shipped to the growing edge of the colony away from the plant, while phosphorus needs to be transported back in the other direction.
Like other coenocytic fungi, AM fungi have aseptate hyphae, meaning that the organism can be considered a fully open pipe system, while they are growing.
The mechanisms that could explain such bidirectional transport in a network of open pipes are so far unknown.

Our group had previously estimated that, when a single AM fungus was attached to a single plant, the fungal use of carbon for growth was proportional to the flux of phosphorus towards the plant~\cite{bisot_carbon-phosphorous_2025}.
In addition, it has been observed in the past that, when a single AM network was connected to two different plant hosts that differed in their carbon supply, more phosphorus was directed towards the plant that provided more carbon~\cite{kiers_reciprocal_2011}. 

Past research has quantified the flow speed in both directions in networks of different ages~\cite{oyarte_galvez_travelling-wave_2025}.
However, without staining the lipids and/or phosphorus, it is difficult to understand the organization of resource flow across the network and the underlying transport mechanisms.
Resource specific staining can shed light on the distribution of resource flux within the network.
For example, using Nile Red staining and two-photon microscopy, it was found that lipid density varies along the hyphae, from about 24\% of the hyphal volume near the root to about 0.5\% at the front, and that lipid bodies move in both directions, mostly with the cytoplasmic stream~\cite{bago_translocation_2002}.
The role of microtubule tracks in this movement has been proposed, and microtubules have been imaged throughout the network~\cite{timonen_microtubules_2001}, but to our knowledge the link between microtubules and lipid transport has never been established.

Flow speeds along the hyphae are modulated with time and location within the network~\cite{oyarte_galvez_travelling-wave_2025}.
This can be interpreted through a traffic analogy inspired by the observed correlation between betweenness centrality, a typical transport network metric, and flow speeds.
The betweenness centrality is the sum of times a shortest path between arbitrary nodes in a network crosses a particular network edge.
Here, we set the root and tip nodes as the set of source and target nodes.
However, this correlation lacks a direct physical basis that explains how such a relationship between global metrics and flows could emerge mechanically.
Because AM fungi are coenocytic, the colony can be represented as a hydraulic network of connected pipes with varying radii.
This hydraulic coupling between all elements of the network could generate spatial correlation in ways that can be physically framed.
Such a mechanical framework, validated against measurements, for growing AM colonies is still missing.

What mechanisms explain the bidirectional transport of resources in the mycorrhizal colony and its modulation over space and time?
How can the transport mechanisms in place maintain a form of bidirectional transport in which the magnitudes of resource flux in opposite directions are maintained proportional? 
To answer these questions, we acquired image data at two different spatial and temporal resolutions to quantify the flow speed in specific edges of growing networks.
At high spatial and temporal resolution, we acquired bright-field videos of transport dynamics within single hyphal edges over multiple days.
Using Nile Red, a neutral lipid-specific fluorescent dye, we also characterized and quantified the movement of carbon in the form of lipids along these same edges.
At low spatial and temporal resolution, we obtained time-lapse images of growing networks that were transformed into their equivalent mathematical graph.
In this way, we can relate the growth of the network with the measured flow speeds observed in the same network, allowing us to explain high-resolution observations both mechanistically and quantitatively.
Additionally, a small number of perturbation experiments were performed to characterize each flow direction.
From these data and findings, we construct a minimal model that explains the observed bidirectional transport of resources in the colony and its modulation over space and time.

\section*{Results}
\namedsubsection{Asymmetric bidirectional speeds}{subsec:res_speeds}

Fungal networks grow through the extension of fungal tips away from the root of the host plant.
Over time, these elongated hyphae develop into a branched mycelial structure.
The network's topological context therefore varies along the length of a hypha.
Close to a growing tip, the network is more tree-like: 40\% of the edges within 1~mm of a tip are bridges, that is, not part of any loop, versus 11--14\% of the edges further than 2~mm from a tip (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}A, inset).

We asked how this change in topological context affected the speeds of the flows in the two directions along a hypha: towards the growing tip (tipward) and towards the root (rootward), as defined by the growth direction of the network.
We imaged flows at high resolution in 731 bright-field videos along the hyphae of five colonies (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:video_positions}) and, mostly at the same positions, in 625 videos of Nile Red fluorescence; for the example network of Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}, the videos were taken over three consecutive days.
We chose runner hyphae, the main routes to the root and the edges of high betweenness centrality~\cite{kirkley_betweenness_2018}, where the tipward and rootward flows could be distinguished by eye (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_video_selection}; SI Fig.~\ref{fig:bc_selection}). 

In undisturbed hyphae (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_lid}), the two flows are superimposed in the video. In a kymograph, the moving particles leave oriented, anisotropic structures whose orientation gives the speed of the particles (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}D); the extraction of the speeds is described in the SI (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_speeds}; SI Fig.~\ref{fig:speed_flux_extraction_real_data}).
In the histogram of the extracted speeds from a video, the tipward speeds (positive) and the rootward speeds (negative) each form a peak.
We took the median of each of the two groups as the speed of that direction in that video (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}E). 
The tipward speeds slow down near the tip, but stay close to 3~$\mu m / s$ further away (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}E).
Rootward speeds generally increase with distance from the tip (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}E), and have a wide range: the median interquartile range of a hypha's speeds is 1.6~$\mu m / s$ rootward, against 0.6~$\mu m / s$ tipward (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:speed_iqr}).
This result was confirmed by repeating a similar experiment on various plates over 15 repetitions over 5 plates showing a consistent phenomenology (Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries} A, B).

In the past, pressure gradients and molecular motors have been suggested to be potential mechanisms driving flows in fungal hyphae~\cite{steinberg_cell_2017,kokkoris_mycelial_2026}. 
It is also important to discuss other potential mechanisms that could drive flows in fungal hyphae, and to evaluate whether our data support or contradict each of them. 
Diffusion as a mechanism could be present close to the tips, as that is where we observe the most random movement of particles (Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}D4), but the directed, unbroken flow in the rest of the network is not consistent with diffusion alone. 
Singular mass flow, as suggested by previous work~\cite{heaton_growth-induced_2010,lew_mass_2005,lew_how_2011}, is also unlikely to be the main driver of flows in our system, as it cannot explain the presence of two opposing streams at similar speeds in the same hypha (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_mass_flow}). 
Peristaltic flow has been observed and studied in \textit{Physarum}~\cite{alim_mechanism_2017}, but involves flow reversals that are not observed in our system when atmospheric pressure and moisture are kept constant (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_lid}). {\color{blue} is it helpful to remark that periodic radial variations are observed in 
\textit{Physarum}, but at the resolution we can image we do not observe such radial oscillations?}

The difference in flow behavior, both within an edge and along an edge, with distance to tip suggests that flow in each direction could be associated with a different mechanism.
An ideal experiment to distinguish these two potential drivers would be to modify the pressure gradient across the network and see how it affects flows.
This experiment has been performed in the past by creating an extracellular osmotic gradient~\cite{lew_mass_2005}.
Following a similar approach, we added a drop of either dissolved  NaCl or sucrose solution (50 to 400 mOsm) (Methods, \nameref{subsec:perturbations}) to the base of the network a few centimeters behind the growing front and imaged near the growth edge (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}A).
We expected that this would create a low turgor pressure within the hyphae at the location of the drop, shifting the pressure gradient within the network, and therefore changing flows internal to the hyphae.
We observed that the flow speed in the tip direction was almost unaffected by the addition of the salt solution  (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}A);  fluorescence imaging of the lipids showed that their movement in the tip direction was also unaffected (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:salt_shock}D).
By contrast, the flow speed in the root direction was approximately increased 3.5 times and relaxed in a minute.
Medium-sized drops of NaCl or sucrose solution resulted in what looked like light hypo-osmotic shocks, pushing the flow towards the tips.

Past work proposed that plant host transpiration could be the driving force explaining the flow of resources towards the root~\cite{kikuchi_aquaporin-mediated_2016}.
Although the plant in our root-organ culture does not produce leaves and, therefore, does not evapotranspirate, we tested whether the connection to the plant was necessary for the flows in the two directions to be maintained.
In one network, we imaged a runner hypha at multiple positions from root to tip to measure the velocity profile along its length.
We then severed the network from the root with a scalpel by cutting along its entire bottom (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}B) and recorded the same positions again, in the first half hour after the cut and then again in the second half hour.
Flows continued in both directions.
Within 5~min of the cut the rootward stream slowed by about a fifth (median ratio 0.78 per trail edge), while the tipward speed stayed at about 3~$\mu m/s$ (ratio 0.96; Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}B).
By 30~min the rootward stream had recovered near the tip (ratios of 1.06--1.11 at about 5~mm from the tip) but not far from it (0.52--0.56 at 23--28~mm), and the asymmetry between the two directions remained throughout.
This matches earlier work showing that flows can still be active for a year without a plant host~\cite{kleinCytoplasmicFlowDynamics2026}.
A connection to the root is therefore not needed for either flow, which rules out transpiration by the plant as their driver.
How far a driving force can reach is a separate question, and we asked it by further shrinking the connected network.

To further focus on the range of a potential driving force, we wanted to know what the minimal size of the subgraph was that was necessary to conserve flows in the two directions.
We made targeted cuts to isolate small segments from the network and image flows at sufficient distance from the cut-induced stress (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}C).
In patches of network as small as only a few millimeters in total length, we still observed flows in the two directions (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}C).
We observed that the flow speed of the lipids in both directions was affected, slowing down about 20\%, but the flow never stopped (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}C).
In none of the experiments did the flow stop.
These experiments demonstrate that the mechanical origin of flows in the two directions does not require long range connectivity of the network to operate (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_segment_size}).

\namedsubsection{Our hypothesis on the flow mechanics, given the evidence}{subsec:res_hypothesis}

For tipward flows, evidence points to a mechanism that is insensitive to pressure gradients and capable of maintaining a speed that is modulated by both hyphal diameter and distance to tip, but is otherwise robust to perturbations (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_forward_flow}).
The lack of response to osmotic shocks, the lack of response to root cuts, and little response to segment isolation all suggest that the mechanism is not pressure driven and that there is something local to the hypha that is responsible for the tipward flows.
We have ruled out diffusion, mass flow, and peristaltic streaming in the previous section as drivers of network-wide flows, and the evidence is consistent with cargo carried by molecular motors along microtubules, which are present throughout AM fungal hyphae~\cite{timonen_microtubules_2001}.

This hypothesis is supported by the characteristics of its likely cargo, lipid droplets.
In some way, these lipid droplets have to arrive at the tips to facilitate growth.
These lipid droplets are believed to be transported by molecular motors in AM fungi~\cite{bago_translocation_2002}, it has been shown that other fungal species transport lipids along microtubules~\cite{guimaraes_peroxisomes_2015, lin_active_2016}, and the speed of the tipward flows is consistent with the range of speeds of molecular motors~\cite{steinberg_cell_2017}.
They move at a steady speed, in a straight line. 
However, flows towards the root are sensitive to pressure changes from osmotic shocks (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}A).
While they are affected by segment isolation, they are still able to flow, indicating that whatever is driving the flows in this direction is not dependent on long range connectivity of the network.
The flows towards the root are also more variable in speed.
This suggests that the mechanism driving these flows is different from the mechanism driving the tipward flows and is likely pressure driven.

We synthesize the above results into a minimal model that explains the observed bidirectional transport of resources in the network and its modulation over space and time.
Namely that motors carry a condensate of lipid droplets in water towards the tips, and that this movement of volume creates a local pressure gradient that drives a return flow towards the root (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}ABC).
This model is consistent with the observed flows in both directions, and with the perturbation experiments that we performed.

As this condensate moves towards the tips throughout the aseptate mycelium, it could drag cytoplasmic fluid along by viscous friction and, because no water can leave the hyphae, push an equal volume back towards the root.
As such, the condensate, a water-oil-like emulsion of lipid droplets of higher viscosity, forms a layer that uniformly coats the internal surface of the hyphal pipe.
We assume the speed profile of the condensate layer in its cross-section is uniform, which can be explained by the effective viscosity, and the drag of the condensate layer on the water in the core of the hyphae.
The condensate layer has a volumetric proportion $\theta$ of lipids and $(1-\theta)$ of water, and moves at a speed $v_m$.
In this model, we also imagine that the condensate layer is coating the walls, and that the water in the core of the hyphae is free to flow at a rate of $q_{w,r<a}$, with a radius $a$ smaller than the hyphal radius $b$.
The volumetric proportion of lipids in the whole pipe is $\Theta$, which is related to $\theta$ by $\Theta = \theta (b^2 - a^2) / b^2$.

By solving the Navier-Stokes equation in the inner pipe, setting the boundary conditions to be $v_m$ at the border of the inner pipe, one can determine the radial speed profile $u(r)$ in cylindrical symmetry to be 

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:speed_profile_main}
u(r) = v_m + \frac{2(q_{w,r<a} - \pi a^2 v_m)(a^2 - r^2)}{\pi a^4}.
\end{equation}

The flow of water through the inner pipe can be written as

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:net_inner_flow}
q_{w,r<a} = q_w - \pi b^2 \Theta \frac{1-\theta}{\theta} v_m,
\end{equation}

where $q_w$ is the net flow of water through the whole pipe.
Interestingly, when one assumes $q_w = 0$ as would be the case in a closed pipe system, we have:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:max_speed}
u(0) = -v_m\left(1+2\frac{\Theta(1-\theta)}{\theta-\Theta}\right) .
\end{equation}

This result suggests a possible explanation for the bidirectional movement that we observe.
For all values of $\Theta$ and $\theta$, we have $u(0)$ which is of opposite sign compared to $v_m$.
It also suggests a possible explanation for the spatial and temporal variation of speeds.
The spatial variation of $\Theta$ (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:theta_spatial}) indeed directly contributes to the magnitude of the rootward speed.
Where a larger share of the lipid moves to the tip, the rootward stream is faster (Spearman $\rho = 0.40$ within plates, also after accounting for tip distance), as the model predicts (Eq.~\ref{eq:max_speed}: the backflow rises with $\Theta$).

Our conceptual model seemed to be supported by the data at the individual pipe level.
Cutting a segment out of the network brings the nearest tip closer, which in the model lowers the motor speed and with it the backflow, as observed after section cuts, where both streams slowed by about a fifth (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}C).
However, we wanted to know what its consequences would be at the network level.
Could it explain the observed variation of speed in space and time?
When establishing the speed profile through the pipe, we were also able to derive the pressure gradient along the idealized hyphal pipe.
Because fluid movement happens at low Reynolds numbers in our system and the entrance length in each individual hyphal pipe is close to its diameter, the approximation of an infinite length cylinder we used to derive flow profile in a single pipe is still justified in the network context.
A network of connected pipes is then analogous to a network of these individual circuit elements.

Such an electrical circuit equivalent of the network can be solved with linear algebra using Kirchhoff's law at each intersection, and requiring that the sum of pressure drops around loops in the network be zero (Methods). 
As in the case of growth-induced mass flow (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_mass_flow}), we set the sink magnitude at each node to correspond to newly created volume $\Delta V_k$ and added a global reservoir, connected to manually annotated nodes which are strongly suspected to be connected to roots.
This volume can be thought of as the minimal volume which needs to be transported to the tips to have sustained growth.
Using lipid dry mass measurements, we can estimate the volume amount of lipid $J_l$ that needs to be transported to the tips to sustain growth (Methods, \nameref{subsec:si_lipid_fraction}).
This allows us to set the sink magnitude at each node in the network.
Additionally, we assume that the network is interested in the energy minimization of the flow, which is equivalent to minimizing the total power dissipation in the network.
This allows us to solve for the pressure at each node in the network, and therefore the demand $J_l$ through each edge.

In each edge the lipid current fixes the size of the motor-driven condensate: with a lipid share $\theta$ of the condensate (set to 0.05) moving at the motor speed $v_m$, $J_l = \pi(b^2 - a^2)\theta v_m$, so the core radius follows from $a^2 = b^2 - J_l/(\pi v_m \theta)$, so an edge that carries more lipid has a thicker condensate and a narrower core.
The sign of $J_l$ determines the direction of motor flow.
The condensate and the core it drags along carry a volume 

\begin{equation}
q_s = \pi v_m\left[(1-\theta)(b^2-a^2) + a^2\right]
\end{equation}

towards the tips.
Each edge then acts as a source of flux $q_s$ in parallel with the hydraulic conductance of its core, $K = \pi a^4/(8\mu L)$, so that its net water flux is $q_w = q_s - K\Delta p$ (Methods), where $\Delta p = p_j - p_i$ is the pressure difference along the edge from node $i$ to node $j$, the direction in which $q_s$ and $q_w$ are counted. 
These values are all derived from the previously solved $J_l$, and we use them in a second solver to find the backflow.
Solving these elements together for the pressures, with no water entering or leaving the network except at the roots, which are tied to a common reservoir (growth is neglected here, as it moves far less volume than the motors), gives $q_w$ on every edge, and with it the pressure-driven return through the core, $q_c = q_s - q_w$. 
We compare the mean speed of this return, $|q_c|/(\pi a^2)$, with the measured rootward speed.
Centerline speed $u(0)$ would be the speed at the center of the pipe, which we do not expect to measure reliably.
The model uses only measured or physical inputs besides the estimated lipid concentration $\theta$: the network and its growth from the time-lapse, hyphal diameters from the network images recalibrated on the filmed hyphae, the measured tipward speed, and $\theta = 0.05$ with $a \geq 0.8\,b$.
While $\theta$ is fitted to the rootward speeds, it also fits within established ranges of lipid fraction in hyphae~\cite{bago_translocation_2002, bago_tracking_2002} (Methods, \nameref{subsec:si_lipid_fraction}).
In the model, $v_m$ is given by a calibrated function of the network distance $d$ to the nearest growing tip, $\hat{v}_m(d) = v_\infty - (v_\infty - v_{\mathrm{tip}})\,e^{-d/\ell}$, fitted to the measured tipward speeds, with $v_\infty = 3.10~\mu$m/s, $v_{\mathrm{tip}} = 2.14~\mu$m/s and $\ell = 1.71$~mm (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig5_plug}b; Methods).
The model predicts the rootward speed on 1,544 bright-field edges in 15 sessions with a median fold error of 1.45. 

A number of properties can be shown in toy networks (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model} D-G).
Tree networks aggregate backflow into their central trunk, increasing flow rate (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}D).
This increase in flow rate can increase backflow speeds, if not modulated by a greater flow capacity, which can be done by increasing the diameter (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}E).
These two properties in tree-like networks, combined with the increase in motor speeds away from the tip, are the basis for the observed rise in rootward speed with distance from the tip (Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}B).
Demand increases it, and diameter has a dampening effect.
One feature this model also predicts is circularity in added loops, specifically if these have a resistance imbalance.
A length or diameter difference between two arms of a loop can create a circular flow, with a faster arm returning to the trunk and a slower arm carrying a net flow to the tip (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}F).
This also concentrates the backflow in the wider or shorter arm.
Finally, as tips grow, they increase their demand for lipid, which increases the motor streaming and with it the backflow (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}G).
These toy models were constructed with typical measured values of hyphal diameter, tipward speed, and tip distance, and show that the model can illustrate the mechanism in backflow speed variation across the network.

The model allows us to predict on each edge what the net volumetric rate of fluid movement $q_w$ would be and then the direction and magnitude of the flow speed in the inner tube of each edge of the network (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig5_plug}).
We found that our model captures salient features of the bidirectionality of flow observed across all edges.
One important result is that we are able to capture the distribution of rootward speeds across the network (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig5_plug}A), with 77.5\% of the 1,544 measured rootward speeds within a factor of 2 of the modelled speeds, and a median ratio of modelled to measured speed of 1.06. This was achieved by fitting only one parameter ($\theta$; SI, \nameref{subsec:si_model_variants}) to the measured rootward speeds, and only using physical inputs and the measured tipward speeds.

Other model candidates have also been tried out, including one that assumes zero flux within each edge individually, meaning that the pressure field only affects one hypha at a time.
This would be the case if switching hyphae at junctions carried a resistance penalty.
The range of backflow speed that can be obtained from such a model failed to encompass the fast rootward speeds measured in high edge-betweenness edges (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_model_variants}). 
The model also captures the rise in rootward speed with distance from the nearest growing tip (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig5_plug}D), which is a key feature of the observed flows (Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}B).
An important element of this rise is also the tipward speed falloff, which was estimated with a hierarchical Bayesian model (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig5_plug}B\@; SI, \nameref{subsec:si_forward_flow}).
Along the filmed trails, the model predicts which trails carry faster rootward flow (Spearman $\rho = 0.50$ between modelled and measured trail medians, 30 trails, $p = 0.005$; Fig.~\ref{fig:fig5_plug}C), and the rise in rootward speed away from the tip has the same sign in 11 of 14 trails, although the modelled rise is shallower than the measured one.

A number of other correlations also emerge from the model (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_tree_loop}; SI Fig.~\ref{fig:relations}). 
The network structure is a key determinant of the rootward speed, with the model predicting that edges with a higher betweenness centrality have a faster backflow.
The model also predicts that edges with a larger share of lipid moving to the tip have a faster backflow, which is consistent with the observed correlation between lipid share and rootward speed (Spearman $\rho = 0.40$ within plates, also after accounting for tip distance).
We also define a measure of edge network density or robustness $\kappa$ as the number of cuts needed to disconnect the network section within 0.25 to 1~mm of an edge from the root, averaged over these section sizes.
The model does not reproduce the observed rise of the rootward speed with $\kappa$.
Splitting the filmed edges at the median $\kappa$ and edge betweenness, the model under-predicts on well-connected edges of low betweenness (3.0 against 3.8~$\mu$m/s measured) and over-predicts on poorly connected edges of high betweenness (4.5 against 3.5~$\mu$m/s).
The model concentrates the return on the main routes to the root, whereas the measured rootward speed is also high in well-connected parts of the network that lie off these routes (SI Figs.~\ref{fig:tree_loop},~\ref{fig:kappa}).

Some other shortcomings are that the model assumes a uniform flow within hyphae, and does not account for the existence of observed sparse flows (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_flow_regimes}).
These are edges where only sparse particles are seen moving in either direction.
The model assumes full flow in all hyphae, and cannot describe these phenomena. 

Hyphal diameter is also a key determinant of the rootward speed, with the model predicting that edges with a larger diameter experience more demand, and therefore have a faster backflow.
Tipward speeds rise with about the square root of the video-extracted diameter, rootward speeds about linearly.

This result was obtained with the video-extracted diameters, which are more accurate than the diameters from the 2x network images.

Even our estimate of 5\% of the cytoplasm filled with lipids suggests that a significant proportion of the volume of the hyphal is filled with lipids.
Such a proportion could easily lead to clogging if all of the lipids coalesce into a single phase.
What then is this large fraction of neutral lipid packaged within the cytoplasm?
The minimal unit of lipid packaging is generally thought to be membrane bound lipid droplets.
Such lipid droplets are generally kept from coalescing by coating~\cite{thiam_why_2017}.
The ensemble of those lipid droplets can then form an emulsion with a set lipid volume fraction.
The emulsion phase, due to the repulsive strength between the different lipid droplets, can have different rheological properties than the rest of the cytoplasm~\cite{jeffrey_rheological_1976}.
We assume that this emulsion is phase-separated from the bulk cytoplasm.

Our simple physical model can therefore explain a wide variety of our experimental observations.
At the single hypha level, it provides an explanation for bidirectional fluid flow.
At the growing network level, it is compatible with the observed non-trivial spatio-temporal variations in flow speeds.
Such a physical explanation of bidirectional transport in AM fungal networks has direct consequences on symbiotic trade by construction.
Because the movement of lipids generates fluid flow in the reverse direction, the proportionality of resource exchange could emerge directly from this physical coupling. 

Our model is by design a closed hydraulic circuit, where the volume returning into the roots equals the motor stream leaving them.
If phosphorus is taken up at the tips, where the condensate turns back, at a concentration [P], the phosphorus delivered to the plant is [P] times the motor volume stream and the carbon carried out is $\theta$ times the motor stream times the carbon density of lipid.
The exchange ratio $C : P = \theta c_{lipid} / [P]$ is then set by two local quantities and does not depend on network size, shape or growth rate: proportional trade follows from the transport itself, without regulation.

\section*{Discussion}

\subsection*{Measured flows and how far they can be trusted}
In this work, we have shown extensive analysis of flows in the hyphal network of AM fungi.
We show that in most hyphae, there are at least two opposing streams: a tipward stream narrowly around 3~$\mu$m/s, slower near the tips, and a rootward stream that is more variable and rises with distance from the tip (1,544 bright-field edges in 15 sessions on 5 plates).
We also imaged lipid movement using Nile Red staining, and found that the lipid moves mostly tipward (median direction bias 0.71, 84\% of edges tipward), while bright-field movement is roughly even.

In trying to differentiate the mechanisms behind these two streams, we found that an osmotic shock changes only the rootward stream, which is therefore pressure-driven.
The tipward stream, which carries most of the lipid, is insensitive to the shock. We interpret it as a lipid-containing condensate carried by molecular motors, but we did not test the motors directly; blocking microtubules or motors and checking whether the tipward stream stops, and the rootward stream with it, would be the decisive experiment.
After a root cut both streams continue, so neither needs the plant.
Isolated segments of a few millimeters keep both streams, about a fifth slower, so both are driven locally. 

For most of the analysis, we rely on bright-field imaging, where the 20 Hz framerate allowed adequate sampling space to capture all the flows.
Because the lid had to stay on during imaging to avoid flows reacting to air flow (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_lid}), a lot of optical resolution was lost between the hyphae and the objective.
This is exacerbated in fluorescence imaging, where the exposure requirements forced us to image most of those videos at 2 or 1 Hz.
This put limits on the velocity sample space (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_forward_choices}; Methods, \nameref{subsec:fl_detection_limit}), so a thorough bright-field to fluorescence comparison could not be done directly.
% NOTE (17 Oct): Removed from the text: 'Perhaps we will add more results from 3D confocal later' (the confocal theta measurement is parked until after submission). 

We also have to be careful about extrapolating the model results to the entirety of the network graph, as there were biases in how the videos were made (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_video_selection}).
High edge-betweenness edges were favoured to be filmed as opposed to low edge-betweenness edges.
The model itself also does not account for the sparse flow regime that was observed, where single particles are seen to be moving, not a large co-moving amount.
Finally, the hyphal diameter extraction, while effective in aggregate, is noisy and brought a lot of noise into the model.
Extracting hyphal diameter from the 2x network images requires us to guess at a diameter of a sub-optical resolution object under varying focus qualities (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_widths}).
While some aspects of the model were validated with the more accurate diameters extracted from videos, full network diameter data cannot be extracted in such a way, and therefore that limits the analysis. 

\subsection*{The model: what it explains and where it falls short}

With these data, we were able to present a model in which motors carry a lipid-containing condensate along the hyphal wall towards the tips, and the displaced fluid returns through the network as a pressure-driven flow.
Both the insensitivity to pressure of the motor-carried stream and the whole pressure field are important to capture the breadth of tipward and rootward measurements.
Key model characteristics like demand dependence (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}D), to a lesser extent diameter dependence (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}E), concentration of backflow into the better-conducting arm of a loop (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}F) and backflow increases due to growth (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}G) were captured in the measured data as well. 

The perturbation experiments are also explained by the broader implications of the model.
An osmotic shock induces a sharp negative osmotic pressure at its application, causing a rush that dies down after a while.
The model has a root component, which merely serves as a large passive reservoir, replicating the idea that a root connection can take in the backflow without having to return it, but the severance of the root does not stop flows.
It does have implications for a rootless organism in the sense that the entire model bases itself on this tipward and rootward axis.
If there is no root, then on a large time-scale, the network could lose this orientation.
It has been shown AM fungal networks can still survive with myristate~\cite{kleinCytoplasmicFlowDynamics2026} in the medium, but the broader model implications are not explored here or there.
A series of cuts isolating a small segment also brings the flows down, because the tip is suddenly moved much closer to the point of measurement, leading to lower motor movement, leading to lower rootward movement.
This is assuming that tip slowdown is a combination of friction and bunching near a network terminus, and not connected to local anatomy that actively sets motor speed. 

In broader context, AM fungi trade with their plant hosts for carbon, and have been shown to have preference for favorable exchange rates~\cite{kiers_reciprocal_2011,bisot_carbon-phosphorous_2025}.
The model in this work as shown, could be a starting point to think of this preference not as an active process, but a result of physical processes in a flow network.
We can recreate the proportionality by thinking of P as being harvested by fungal tips as the network grows into the soil.
If this proportionality is to match the measured exchange rate of Bisot et al.~\cite{bisot_carbon-phosphorous_2025} (3--4 $\mu$g C per $\mu$g P by mass), it is important to note that the model predicts phosphorus in proportion to transported carbon, not to the carbon used for growth (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_cp_exchange}).
This distinction is testable.
The rootward stream is fast enough: at 3--4 $\mu$m/s it covers 1~cm in $\sim$45~min, consistent with $\geq$85\% of absorbed phosphorus reaching the root within $\sim$100~h~\cite{bisot_carbon-phosphorous_2025}.
One way to package the phosphate at such a high concentration is as polyphosphate, which has been shown to exist in AM fungi~\cite{hijikata_polyphosphate_2010,kikuchi_aquaporin-mediated_2016}.

We see that bidirectional resource movement and trade could be largely mechanically mediated.
Such a control mechanism has the advantage of being robust because the proportionality of flow is embedded in the physical properties of the system and, therefore, does not require additional biological control.
However, in our model, the path taken by the phosphorus in the network is strongly dependent on its relative cumulative hydraulic resistance and, therefore, on the edge radii.
Radial adaptation could constitute a flow control mechanism.
However, we never observed hyphal external radii diminishing.
Full control would probably require an additional ingredient of radial shrinkage. 

Where the model does fall short is its overprediction on high edge-betweenness sections, and underprediction on low edge-betweenness edges which are deep inside the network.
Some high edge-betweenness hyphae are instead under-predicted, which we attribute to faults in the network extraction: because the pressure is solved over the whole network, a wrongly connected or missing hypha lets the solve pool the backflow in neighbouring edges rather than in the hypha that carries it (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_rerouting}).
Additionally, with enough of a conductance imbalance, we can see pressure flows move in the same direction of the motor flows due to recirculation.
We have seen no clear case of this.
Only a pressure field spanning the whole network concentrates backflow into central hyphae and reproduces the high measured rootward speeds. 

Finally, the model is hampered by its noisy diameter measurements.
Every comparison with video-extracted diameters shows that much stronger correlations can be found if diameters were extracted with more precision and accuracy.
Future work has to see how this can be done while also imaging the growth of an entire network (SI, \nameref{subsec:si_widths}; SI, \nameref{subsec:si_model_variants}). 

One element that the model does not take into account is the observed phenomenon of sparse flows.
These are regions with sparse particles moving through the hyphae, without evidence of being moved by back-to-back traffic.
These occur mostly in poorly connected (low-$\kappa$), low edge-betweenness regions of the network, and are likely the result of both low demand and routing for motors, and low pressure differences.
This points to the idea that the cytoplasm could be a yield stress fluid, which would have a different rheology than the condensate layer.
Sparse particles could still be moving with motors along microtubules, just not as much as in central hyphae. 

\subsection*{Relation to other systems and to the symbiosis}

% SUGGESTED (20 Oct, Simon): Coco (ideas; graph extraction pipelines): review and extend (with references) this subsection and "Growth may follow supply": the oriented track graph, Kirchhoff violations, supply-driven growth and the information bottleneck.
Our results suggest that the lipids preferentially move in a direction informed by fungal growth.
Taking the steady $\sim$3~$\mu$m/s tipward stream as the motor-carried condensate, on loop edges, where the direction is not fixed by the tree structure, its direction matches the growth direction on 86\% of 677 edges.
It is likely that part of the network of transport filaments simply orients in the direction of tip growth at the moment of its creation and persists in that direction over longer timescales.
The tipward stream's insensitivity to pressure, its locality and its steady speed are consistent with the condensate holding this lipid being carried by molecular motors that move in one direction on polar cytoskeletal filaments.
This network of filaments inside the fungal network is mathematically equivalent to a weighted oriented graph, where the weight of a given edge can represent the number of cytoskeletal filaments, allowing molecular motor movement.
It is unclear how this oriented network organizes to maintain a coherent intensity and polarity with growth. 

Two network solutions to long-distance transport have been described in other eukaryotes: pressure-driven peristaltic streaming in \textit{Physarum}~\cite{alim_mechanism_2017} and motor transport on microtubules in the reticulate amoeba \textit{Filoreta ramosa}~\cite{guest_multiple_2026}.
In \textit{Filoreta}, nuclei, lysosomes and mitochondria move in both directions along microtubules at 5--8~$\mu$m/s, and their speed rises with branch diameter, which the authors link to wider branches holding more microtubules~\cite{guest_multiple_2026}.
Our tipward stream likewise rises with diameter (as $D^{0.48}$; SI, \nameref{subsec:si_forward_flow}), whereas the rootward stream rises about twice as steeply (as $D^{1.07}$).
AM fungi would thus combine both solutions, motors in one direction and pressure in the other.
The comparison has limits: \textit{Filoreta} is a wall-less amoeba without turgor, and it moves single organelles rather than a lipid condensate.

One way of thinking about the system is that it is mainly driven by supply.
Although in this paper our approach was to estimate what the flux of carbon was in each edge of the network from growth observation, it is possible that from the fungal point of view things work the other way around.
Growth would mainly emerge from the network's underlying molecular motor track organization.

This has the advantage of solving an informational bottleneck.
If a tip grows at one end of the network, how is the information about its carbon demand transmitted to the root-fungal interface where that carbon will eventually come from?
Instead of needing information transmission to match supply and demand and orient carbon fluxes accordingly, fungal tips could grow and branch according to the carbon supply of the existing network of molecular motor tracks.
Indeed, the net flux of lipid across the colony could be entirely determined by its orientation (track direction) and intensity (number of parallel tracks assuming tracks are saturated with motors) as well as by the plant supply (source).
The growth might then simply occur at a rate limited by the network-determined supply of lipids.

However, some abrupt topological changes, such as anastomosis or the colony abandoning growth in a specific region of space, could require a reorganization of the cytoskeletal filaments.
It is unclear how that reorganization is modulated in a way that allows one to maintain a flux of carbon that is consistent with the general fungal growth strategy.
Depending on the timing of the reorganization, it is also likely that the flux of lipids moving on microtubules will not always follow the Kirchhoff law at some intersection.
Because lipids are incompressible and cannot accumulate in a node, this means that they will be recirculated in the internal cytoplasmic flow.
This phenomenon could explain the observation that lipids can also move in two directions in fungal hyphae.
In \textit{Filoreta}, Guest and Dawson infer antiparallel microtubule arrays where branches growing from opposite directions fused~\cite{guest_multiple_2026}, another lineage in which anastomosis would leave tracks pointing both ways in one branch.

In our model, we assumed that water could not flow out of the hyphae except at the hyphal-root interface.
However, AM fungi express functional aquaporins~\cite{li_first_2013,kikuchi_aquaporin-mediated_2016}, which could let water in and out along the hyphae.
Any water potential gradient between the inside and outside of the hyphae could then produce influx or outflux of water at any point of the network.
The resulting system would be analogous to the xylem-phloem system of plants.
In plants, the sugar loaded phloem generates an influx of water from the xylem.
% NOTE (18 Oct): the phloem (Münch) analogy has no citation; none is in PhD.bib yet (e.g. Knoblauch et al. 2016, eLife, or Jensen et al. 2016, Rev Mod Phys).
This in turn generates a net flux of water through the phloem.
Any kind of osmolyte that would be produced inside the fungal network could very well drive a similar osmotic pump by changing the overall flow distribution in the network.
Kikuchi et al.\ found that suppressing host transpiration slowed the translocation of polyphosphate through AM hyphae towards the host, and proposed transpiration-driven hyphal water flow as its driver~\cite{kikuchi_aquaporin-mediated_2016}.
In our root-organ cultures there is no transpiration, yet both streams continue, also after the root is cut (Fig.~\ref{fig:perturbations}B); the motor-driven return proposed here would act without a shoot, alongside transpiration where there is one.

Such effects may play a role in the transport of resources within the AM colony; they would add to the mechanism of bidirectional flow described here rather than replace it.
Regardless of the contribution of other mechanisms, our results indicate that lipid movement results in some fluid flow in the opposite direction.
Such backflows likely play a significant role in the full picture of AM fungal flows.
The framework developed here can also be flexibly expanded to include the effect of other kinds of carbon demand, such as spore growth or hyphal maintenance.
By adding sources and sinks of water where aquaporins exchange it with the surroundings, it is also possible to account for additional transport mechanisms.

\section*{Conclusion}
In short, we have shown that within AM fungi, a minimal model in which motors carry a lipid-containing condensate towards the tips and a pressure-driven backflow returns cytoplasm to the root recreates the range and dynamics of measured flows. Its limitations are in the noise of the measured parameters, and certain unrepresented flow regimes. One consequence of this model is its physical result of carbon-phosphorus exchange, which in the model can be described as a linear relationship. In the future, phosphorus packing and the role of water in AM fungal transport should be investigated to see further effects. Microtubule dynamics in AM fungi are also an open field to investigate, as they have not been widely imaged in vivo before.
