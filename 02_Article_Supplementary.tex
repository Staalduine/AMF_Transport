\namedsubsection{Selection bias of the filmed hyphae}{subsec:si_video_selection}
% NOTE (10 Oct): The section text is to be written by Simon; the facts are below (sources: transport_paper feat/bc-selection-bias d2054b7, manuscript/bc_selection_bias.md, #130).
% NOTE (1, why): the flow videos were taken by eye on runner hyphae where the two streams could be told apart (main text, Results opening). Speed statistics therefore describe the filmed hyphae, not the network as a whole; this section measures how different the filmed edges are, so the reader can judge how far the results generalise.
% NOTE (2, measure): Betweenness centrality (BC) is used as defined by Oyarte Galvez et al. 2025 (oyarte_galvez_travelling-wave_2025, after freeman_set_1977): for each edge, it counts the shortest paths (by edge length; equal-length paths share 1/sigma each) from every node of the session network to the root compartment, i.e. one super-node joined at zero length to all annotated roots. The count is divided by the number of source nodes, so it is the share of nodes whose route to the root crosses the edge. Edges with BC = 0 are left out (62,469 of 1,139,071; 42 filmed). A variant uses only growing tips as sources.
% NOTE (3, groups): The filmed edges are the store edges that carry a video mapping (1,972 with BC > 0, 15 sessions on 5 plates). The references are all edges of the same session networks (1,076,602) and, to separate selection from where the plates were filmed, the edges inside the convex hull of the filmed positions (310,967).
% NOTE (4, result): Filmed edges sit at a median 82nd percentile of BC within their own network (IQR 54-95); every session's median is above 50 (73-96; Wilcoxon over sessions p = 6e-5). The median log10 BC is -2.40 for filmed edges, -3.80 for all edges and -3.83 inside the hull, so the shift is selection, not spatial extent. Per session, P(filmed > other edges) is 0.63-0.89. Tips-only BC gives the same picture: the 80th percentile (IQR 61-92) and session medians of 64-95; the rank correlation of the two BC variants across edges is 0.78.
% NOTE (5, what else differs): Filmed edges are thicker (store diameter 10.0 against 7.8 um), slightly closer to a growing tip (4.3 against 4.8 mm), and far less often tree-like (15 % against 36 %; 33 % inside the hull). BC and diameter covary, so the diameter difference is not a separate selection.
% NOTE (6, consequence for the speeds, panel C): Among filmed edges, bright-field median speed rises weakly with log BC in both directions: Spearman 0.18 to tip (n 1,706) and 0.18 to root (n 1,555), unchanged with tip distance partialled out, and positive in 12/15 and 14/15 sessions. This is the same direction as in Oyarte Galvez (speeds in both directions rise with BC), but weak. Tips-only BC gives 0.39 / 0.36 raw and 0.21 / 0.25 with tip distance partialled out.
% NOTE (7, caveats): Edges along one hypha share nearly the same BC, so pooled p-values overstate independence (the per-network percentiles are the robust statistic). Edges are counted, not weighted by length (Oyarte's Extended Data Fig 7a is length-weighted). Store diameters are used for both groups (there are no lumen diameters network-wide).

\namedsubsection{Growth-induced mass flow}{subsec:si_mass_flow}
% NOTE (12 Oct): This section was moved from the main text (Results, 'Lipid transport across the network') as it stood, with the outline headings dropped and the first paragraph uncommented; its notes came with it. Its figure references (Fig 4D, Fig 1F,G, SI carbonflow b, d, e) are stale; see the notes.
Hyphal extension as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}D, in addition to consuming carbon, is also associated with the creation of new network volume.
The corresponding demand for water has been shown to be the mechanism driving flows in colonies of other (non-mycorrhizal) fungal species~\cite{heaton_growth-induced_2010, heaton_advection_2012}.
In those systems, the movement of matter within the network is well explained by the movement of water in a network of pipes with set sources and sinks. A hydraulic pressure gradient is established across the network, from inoculation roots to the tips, and the resulting flows are sufficient to explain the observed movement of matter.
We wanted to investigate whether this explanation was compatible with our AMF system.
Because volume creation and carbon demand are proportional, we use the same approach as for carbon, simply replacing the magnitude of the carbon source and sink, $\Delta C_k$, with the volume $\Delta V_k$.
We keep a similar global source node corresponding to a water reservoir. {\color{blue} the word ``similar" suggests that a reader will know the other work which should not be assumed}

We represented the growing network as a temporally evolving graph where each edge has an activation time corresponding to its first appearance.
We call $N$ the set of all graph nodes, and $E$ the set of all graph edges.
This labelling allowed the growth between two time steps to be established in a spatially resolved manner (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}D).
For each growing node $k\in N$ we could establish the total biovolume {\color{blue} maybe ``network volume" since you have not used the term biovolume above} newly built from that node $\Delta V_k$.
The demand for carbon at each node $\Delta C_k$ is proportional to the newly built biovolume. {\color{blue} maybe just write: The nodal demand for carbon  $\Delta C_k\propto\Delta V_k $
} These are modeled as lipid effluxes out of the system, so the demand gets a negative sign. Lipids must be supplied from the root compartment, which is represented by adding one additional "source" node to the network, $S_{source} = - \sum_{k \in N} \Delta C_k$. The "source" node is connected to the nodes of the network closest to the plant (see Methods) through zero length junctions. Inevitably, because of loops created through anastomoses, there are multiple possible paths from the "source" node to a node with non-zero carbon demand. 

These multiple paths require us to resolve ambiguities in the lipid distribution inside the network, while adhering to the growth constraints in the observed data.
We assume, as in previous work \cite{heaton_growth-induced_2010}, that carbon flux would be distributed over those paths in a way that minimizes the dissipation of hydraulic energy. 

We assign to each edge $(i,j)\in E$ a hydraulic resistance $\mathcal{R}_{i,j}$ associated with energy dissipation. {\color{blue} i think in network literature these are called resistances, so maybe write as ${\cal R}_{i,j}$, which are different than energy dissipation} We set $\mathcal{R}_{i,j} = \frac{8 \mu L_{i,j}}{\pi b_{i,j}^4}$ where $L_{i,j}$ is the length of the edge, $b_{i,j}$ is its radius, and $\mu$ is a viscosity that is treated as a constant in global energy minimization.
With a carbon flux from $i$ to $j$ $\phi_{i,j}$ the power dissipation {\color{blue} maybe write as: the rate of energy consumption} is set to $\mathcal{R}_{i,j} \phi_{i,j}^2$.
We minimize the objective function $\sum_{(i,j)\in E} \mathcal{R}_{i,j} \phi_{i,j}^2$ under the constraint that for all nodes $k$, $\sum_{i\in N} \phi_{i,k} = \Delta C_k$ (Methods).
This model allows us to estimate, in each edge of the network, the expected direction and lower bound of lipid flux required for the observed growth of the tip (Fig. SI\ref{fig:carbonflow}b). 

% When comparing these expected lipid fluxes with the one observed in each edge. We found that in 70\% of the cases, the direction of lipid flux could be predicted by our model. This percentage increased to more than 80\% when we considered only points in the network where both the modelled and measured absolute flux were high (Fig. \ref{fig:carbonflow}c).

The calculation  yields the exact volumetric flow rate $Q_{i,j}$ in the hydraulic equivalent of the network.
The speed $v_{i,j}$ was then set as $v_{i,j}=\frac{Q_{i,j}}{\pi b_{i,j}^2}$. {\color{blue} something is wrong with this sentence since model is referred to twice:} We found that this model did not explain the speed in the direction predicted by the model (Fig. SI\ref{fig:carbonflow}d).
Such a model also does not explain the existence of fluid movement in the other direction.
Indeed, the fluid flow always follows the pressure gradient and therefore cannot be bidirectional.
% NOTE (7 Oct): Fig. SI carbonflow (d, e) is one of the wrong SI files (FigS2-S5, transport_paper#129), so the speed claim here and the peaked-distribution claim below have no figure behind them until it is replaced.

Finally, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}F,G and \ref{fig:asymmetries}A, the flow speed in the modeled and observed direction of the lipid flux narrowly peaks around the value $v_0 = 3\mu m/s$.
% NOTE (12 Oct): Fig 1 has panels A-E, so 'Fig. hyphal_profiling F,G' points to nothing; the tipward speed distribution is Fig 2A/B. The motor-speed range (2-6 um/s, howard_mechanics_2002) repeats the hypothesis section (steinberg_cell_2017).
In addition to not being able to predict a speed of the right order of magnitude, the growth induced mass flow model does not yield such a peaked distribution (Fig. SI\ref{fig:carbonflow}e).
The peaked distribution instead strongly suggests that the movement of lipids within the AM fungal colony is an active process driven by the movement of molecular motors on tracks walking at fixed speed.
In fact, the speed of known molecular motors can typically be in the range $2-6\mu m/s$ for the faster ones \cite{howard_mechanics_2002}.
This hypothesis is further motivated by the qualitative observation that the movement of lipids in the colony follows well established tracks. 

\namedsubsection{Lipid volume fraction needed for growth}{subsec:si_lipid_fraction}
% NOTE (12 Oct): This section was moved from the main text (Results, after the mass-flow test) as it stood, with the outline headings dropped (Simon: not central to the paper; mention in the Discussion). Is moving lipids at such high speeds necessary to match supply and demand while avoiding saturating hyphae with lipids?
To answer that question, we set the speed of all lipids at $v_0 = 3~\mu m/s$ and used the value of the lipid fluxes that we estimated from the growth data to estimate the lipid volume fraction $\Theta_{i,j} = \frac{\phi_{i,j}}{v_0 \pi b_{i,j}^2}$ in each edge of the network required to explain the observed growth.
We found that as we moved away from the tip of the hypha, that fraction gradually increased by 10mm {\color{blue} why mm? are you referring to lipid volume fraction?}and reached a value as high as 4\%.

However, in that earlier research the magnitude of the maximum volume fraction was estimated to be $\sim$ 20\%. 
% NOTE (7 Oct, Bago value and key): '~20 %' cites bago_carbon_2000, a review (Plant Physiol 124). The measurement is in bago_translocation_2002 (Plant Physiol 128, Fig. 3): ~24 % of hyphal volume near the root, 0.5 % at the front. The intro (L37) now uses that key and value, so the two disagree.

% NOTE (15 Oct, model): In the main model the lipid volume fraction of the whole lumen is Theta = theta (b^2 - a^2) / b^2 = J / (pi b^2 v), where theta = 0.05 is the lipid share of the motor-driven condensate. \namedsubsection{Modelling the tipward speed}{subsec:si_forward_flow}
% NOTE (15 Oct): The text below was drafted by Claude on Simon's request (fill out the forward model SI section), from the notes of this section and the current fits (transport_paper feat/flow-regime: forward_speed_bayes.py, edges.csv / posterior.csv / width_compare.csv; width_feasibility_stats.csv). The width numbers marked 873 edges are from the walls_v4 rebuild (note of 11 Oct below); the force-balance sentence needs Simon's check of the model's form.
The transport model needs the tipward (motor) speed on every edge of the network (Methods), so we asked which properties of a hypha predict it.
We used the bright-field videos only (\nameref{subsec:si_forward_choices}).
For each edge we pooled the moving tipward streaks of all its videos, leaving out video directions with fewer than 5 streaks and video edges shorter than 20~$\mu$m, which gave 1,481 edges with 299,584 streaks in 15 sessions on 5 plates.

\paragraph*{Distance to the nearest growing tip.}
The tipward speed is lower close to the growing tips and levels off within a few millimetres (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig5_plug}b).
We described this with a falloff to an offset,
\[
v(d) = v_\infty - (v_\infty - v_{\mathrm{tip}})\, e^{-d/\ell},
\]
with $d$ the network distance to the nearest growing tip at the session.
We fitted it with a hierarchical Bayesian model on the log median streak speed of each edge, $y_i = \log v(d_i) + w_p + u_i + \epsilon_i$, with an edge effect $u_i \sim N(0, \sigma_{\mathrm{edge}})$, a plate effect $w_p$ (summing to zero over plates) and a sampling error $\epsilon_i$ whose standard deviation was bootstrapped from the edge's own streaks, so that edges with few or scattered streaks count less.
Priors were uniform on $v_\infty$ and $v_{\mathrm{tip}}$ (0.05 to 10~$\mu$m/s) and on $\log \ell$ (10~$\mu$m to 50~mm), half-normal on the standard deviations, and the posterior was sampled with adaptive Metropolis (4 chains, $\hat{R} \leq 1.01$).
The posterior medians [95\% intervals] were $v_{\mathrm{tip}} = 2.18$ [1.92, 2.39]~$\mu$m/s, $v_\infty = 2.93$ [2.86, 3.03]~$\mu$m/s and $\ell = 1.85$ [1.11, 3.52]~mm.
Edges scatter around the curve by 0.22 on the log scale (about 25\%), and the plates differ by $-6$ to $+7$\%.
The speed at the tip rests on few edges: 82 lie within 1~mm of a tip and 7 within 250~$\mu$m.
In least-squares fits on the edge medians, fitted on four plates and scored on the fifth, the offset form predicted the held-out edges better than a constant speed or an exponential rise from zero (median fold error 1.14, against 1.17 and 1.16), and a step at a fixed distance did worst.
The transport model uses an earlier least-squares fit of the same form ($v_\infty = 3.10$, $v_{\mathrm{tip}} = 2.14~\mu$m/s, $\ell = 1.71$~mm; Methods), whose far-from-tip speed is 6\% above the Bayesian one.

\paragraph*{Hyphal width.}
The tipward speed also rises with the lumen width measured in the same videos (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:width_lumen_speed}), with a within-plate Spearman correlation of 0.54 (1,449 edges).
The relation holds in each of the five plates and is unchanged when the tip distance is accounted for (log-log slope 0.53 [0.46, 0.60], partial within-plate $\rho = 0.53$; 873 edges).
Adding a factor $(w/9~\mu\mathrm{m})^\beta$ to the Bayesian fit (1,434 edges with a lumen width) gave $\beta = 0.49$ [0.45, 0.54] without and $0.47$ [0.43, 0.52] with the tip term; with the width included the tip falloff remains ($v_{\mathrm{tip}} = 2.18$, $v_\infty = 2.92~\mu$m/s) and its length shortens to $\ell = 1.10$ [0.63, 1.98]~mm.
Scored on held-out plates, the predictions from tip distance and width together, and from a force-balance form with a tip throttle, correlated best with the measured speeds (within-plate $\rho = 0.58$), width alone reached 0.50 and tip distance alone 0.22.
The rootward speed rises more steeply with width (slope 1.16 [1.05, 1.27]).
Because the network images do not resolve the lumen width (\nameref{subsec:si_widths}), the transport model uses the tip distance only (\nameref{subsec:si_forward_choices}).

\namedsubsection{Choices in the tipward speed model}{subsec:si_forward_choices}
% NOTE (15 Oct): The text below was drafted by Claude on Simon's request, from the notes of this section and of the section above.
Three choices shape the tipward speed used in the transport model.

\paragraph*{Bright-field only.}
The fits use the bright-field videos (20 frames per second) only.
The Nile Red videos (1 or 2 frames per second) lose fast streaks: relative to 1--3~$\mu$m/s, the number of streaks found per bright-field streak drops to 0.22 at 4--5~$\mu$m/s and 0.11 at 6--7~$\mu$m/s at 2 frames per second, and to 0.07--0.08 at 6--8~$\mu$m/s at 1 frame per second, and the per-pixel medians at 1 frame per second level off at 4.4--4.5~$\mu$m/s (Methods, \nameref{subsec:fl_detection_limit}).
Fluorescence speeds are therefore censored near 5~$\mu$m/s, and we do not compare them with the bright-field speeds.

\paragraph*{A falloff to an offset, not to zero.}
A falloff to zero at the tip, $v(d) = v_\infty (1 - e^{-d/\ell})$, fitted with the same hierarchical Bayesian model, gave $v_\infty = 2.79$ [2.75, 2.83]~$\mu$m/s and $\ell = 194$ [145, 241]~$\mu$m (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:tip_falloff_zero}).
To reach zero at the tip and still pass the edges nearest to it, its falloff length shrinks to about 200~$\mu$m, after which the curve is flat: it reaches 2.0~$\mu$m/s at 250~$\mu$m and 2.8~$\mu$m/s from about 1~mm onwards.
It therefore misses the gradual rise over 1--3~mm and lies about 10\% below the edges beyond 4~mm, while within 500~$\mu$m of a tip it fits as well as the offset form.
By the widely applicable information criterion the offset form predicts better (expected log predictive density higher by 25.5 $\pm$ 7.3), with 10.6 of the difference from the 82 edges within 1~mm of a tip and 12.7 from those beyond 3~mm.
A drop to near zero over the last tens of micrometres, as seen in the hand-traced tip kymographs (\nameref{subsec:si_tips}), is below what the edge data resolve and is not excluded.

\paragraph*{Tip distance, not width.}
Width predicts the tipward speed better than tip distance (\nameref{subsec:si_forward_flow}), but the transport model needs a speed on every edge of the network, and there only the network widths are available.
These track the lumen width poorly (within-plate $\rho$ 0.48--0.61) and correlate with the tipward speed at $\rho$ 0.30--0.36, against 0.54 for the lumen width (\nameref{subsec:si_widths}).
The transport model therefore uses the tip-distance profile alone, with physical inputs only and no fitted width exponent.
A constant motor speed scores worse in the transport model (Methods).

% NOTE (1, tree vs loop, figure 'tree_loop', 1,544 filmed edges: 213 tree (bridge), 1,331 loop): Median tree / loop within 4 mm of a tip and beyond 4 mm. % NOTE (2, robustness kappa, definition): kappa is the fewest hyphae that must be cut to disconnect a section of the network (every node within 500 um along the network of an edge) from the root; by Menger's theorem it is the number of hypha-disjoint routes from the section to the root. Cuts count per arc (junction to junction), computed as a maximum flow on the arc graph. A tree section has kappa 1. The imaged network continues below the bottom edge of the image towards the root compartment, so every node within 1 mm of the bottom edge counts as connected to the root (open bottom); with the solver's 13 or so root nodes alone, kappa is 1 lower in median and its relation to speed is inconsistent between plates.
% NOTE (3, kappa results, figure 'kappa', 1,484 filmed edges): kappa ranges 1-11 (median 4) and rises away from tips (rho +0.30). The measured rootward speed rises with kappa (rho +0.29; +0.24 after removing tip distance, routed demand and width), on 4 of 5 plates within plate (EPY088 +0.25, LPD025 +0.45, PNF080 +0.22, STR112 +0.15, PNF072 +0.05). The model does not follow (rho +0.04; partial +0.03): it over-predicts at kappa 1-2 (median log2 model / measured +0.27) and under-predicts above 4 (-0.12 to -0.13). The section size matters little (250 / 500 / 1,000 um: rho +0.24 / +0.27 / +0.28 with the solver roots).
% NOTE (4, open-bottom model variant, tested and not adopted; Simon: main model unchanged): Making every junction and end within 1 mm of the bottom edge a root of the global solve (58-399 roots per session, model_accuracy_throttle.py --open-bottom 1000) gives 76.7 % of edges within a factor of 2 (main 77.0 %), trail segments 82.8 % (82.2 %), and leaves the kappa trend of the error unchanged; within 3 mm of the bottom edge it lowers the model (0.89 against 0.99 of the measured) and the score (69.7 against 72.6 %). The faster backflow in robust sections is therefore not an artefact of the few roots of the solve.
