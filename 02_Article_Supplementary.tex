\namedsubsection{Tree-like edges and distance to the growing tips}{subsec:si_tree_tip}
% NOTES (updated 11 Nov; TEXT and caption written 13 Nov). CITED FROM: main text Fig 1A (inset, "about 40% within 1 mm against 10-14% beyond 2 mm"), Methods "Tree and loop edges" (\todo for the bins).
% SOURCE: transport_paper Working_notebooks/manuscript_figures/fig1.py, bridge_share_by_tip(); numbers from the 29 Oct rebuild of Figs 1-2 on the current main model (split_even_j10000, 20 um minimum video edge; AMF_Transport PR #12).
% 1 DEFINITION: an edge is tree-like when it is a bridge of the network graph (removing it disconnects the network; networkx bridges on the simple graph, degree-2 nodes dissolved), computed on the network as it was at each session (model tables' `bridge` column); otherwise it lies on a loop.
% 2 EDGE SET: the filmed edges of the main tables, 15 sessions on plates 1-5: 1,778 edges, 1,778 with a tip distance (network distance to the nearest growing tip, d_tip). This is not the 1,544-edge model set; name the set in the text (see tab:static_video_sets).
% 3 RESULT (bins by tip distance: edges, % tree-like): <1 mm 100, 45.0%; 1-2 mm 209, 21.1%; 2-4 mm 499, 10.6%; 4-8 mm 650, 10.6%; >8 mm 320, 12.5%. Main text "about 45%" and "10-13%" match.
% 4 CONTEXT: over all edges of the same networks 36% are tree-like, against 14% of the filmed edges, so the filmed set under-samples tree-like edges (lead into \nameref{subsec:si_video_selection}).
% OPEN: whether to add all edges to the figure and the per-session spread; fill the Methods \todo with these bins.

An edge is \emph{tree-like} when it is a bridge of the network graph with no root of its own beyond it: cutting it splits the network in two, and everything beyond it hangs from that one hypha and draws its water through it.
Everything else lies on a loop, with at least two routes back to the root.
The second half of the condition matters only rarely, in that 98.4\% of the 408,574 bridges of the 15 session networks already satisfy it, but it is what makes the section a tree hydraulically as well as topologically: no net water crosses such an edge, so the model's tree case applies to it exactly (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig6_examples}A).
A bridge with roots on both sides fails it, because the root compartment joins the two sides and water crosses the edge; 10 of the 214 filmed bridges are of that kind.

Tree-like hyphae are a feature of the growing front rather than of the network behind it (Fig.~\ref{fig:tree_like_share}).
Of the filmed edges within 1~mm of a growing tip, 45\% are tree-like.
The share halves over the next millimetre and settles near an eighth from 2~mm outwards, with no further decline.
The whole network falls the same way over the first two millimetres, from 57\% to about a third, and then stays there, so the gradient is a property of the colony rather than of which hyphae we filmed; the filmed edges simply sit below it everywhere beyond the front (Fig.~\ref{fig:tree_like_share}B).
This matches a colony that grows by extending and branching hyphae and closes loops behind the front by fusion.

% 1 WHY: the flow videos were taken by eye on runner hyphae where the two streams could be told apart, so the speed statistics describe the filmed hyphae, not the whole network. This subsection measures how different the filmed edges are, so the reader can judge how far the results generalise.
% 2 MEASURE: root-directed betweenness centrality (BC) as in Oyarte Galvez et al. 2025 (oyarte_galvez_travelling-wave_2025, after freeman_set_1977): per edge, the share of the network's nodes whose shortest path (by length) to the root compartment crosses the edge (roots joined in one super-node; equal-length paths share 1/sigma). Edges with BC = 0 are left out (62,469 of 1,139,071; 42 filmed). Variant: growing tips only as sources.
% 3 GROUPS: filmed edges = store edges with a video mapping, 1,740 with BC > 0 (15 sessions, plates 1-5; a different set from the 1,778 of S1.1, see tab:static_video_sets). References: all edges of the same networks (1,076,602) and, to separate selection from where the plates were filmed, the edges inside the convex hull of the filmed positions (310,967).
% 4 RESULT: filmed edges sit at a median 83rd BC percentile of their own network (IQR 55-95); every session's median is above 50 (73-96; Wilcoxon over sessions p = 6e-5). Median log10 BC: -2.40 filmed, -3.80 all, -3.83 inside the hull, so the shift is selection, not spatial extent. Per session P(filmed > other) 0.63-0.89. Tips-only BC: 80th percentile (IQR 61-92), session medians 64-95; rank correlation of the two variants 0.78.
% 5 WHAT ELSE DIFFERS: filmed edges are thicker (store diameter 10.2 against 7.8 um), slightly closer to a growing tip (4.3 against 4.8 mm) and less often tree-like (14% against 36%; 33% inside the hull). BC and diameter covary, so the diameter difference is not a separate selection.
% 6 CONSEQUENCE (panel C): among filmed edges the bright-field median speed rises only weakly with log BC: Spearman 0.18 tipward (n 1,656) and 0.18 rootward (n 1,510), unchanged with tip distance partialled out, positive in 12/15 and 14/15 sessions; same direction as Oyarte Galvez. Tips-only BC: 0.39 / 0.36 raw, 0.21 / 0.25 after tip distance. So the results describe high-BC hyphae.
% 7 CAVEATS: edges along one hypha share nearly the same BC, so pooled p-values overstate independence (the per-network percentiles are the robust statistic). Edges are counted, not length-weighted (Oyarte's Extended Data Fig 7a is length-weighted). Store diameters for both groups (no lumen diameters network-wide).
The flow videos were not taken at random positions.
They were taken by eye, on runner hyphae where the tipward and rootward streams could be told apart, so the speeds in this paper describe the hyphae we filmed rather than the network as a whole.
This subsection measures how far the filmed hyphae differ from the rest, so that the reader can judge how widely the results carry.

We rank hyphae by root-directed betweenness centrality, as in Oyarte Galvez et al.~\cite{oyarte_galvez_travelling-wave_2025} after Freeman~\cite{freeman_set_1977}.
For each edge it is the share of growing tips whose shortest route to the root node subset runs through that edge, with the roots taken as one subset and equal-length routes sharing the count.
It is a measure of how much of the growing front a hypha serves.
Edges that carry no such route are left out, which leaves 193,387 of the 1,139,071 edges and 888 of the filmed ones.
Those filmed edges are compared against the remaining edges of the same networks and against the 55,936 of them inside the convex hull of the filmed positions, the second comparison separating the choice of hypha from the choice of where on the plate to look.

The filmed hyphae are among the busiest in their networks (Fig.~\ref{fig:bc_selection}A,B).
They sit at a median 82nd percentile of their own network (interquartile range 63--92), every one of the 15 sessions has a median above the 50th, and a filmed edge carries more tip routes than an unfilmed one in 76\% of random pairs.
Against the hull reference the shift is smaller (70\%) and in 6 of the 15 sessions it is not significant, so part of it is where on the plate we looked rather than which hypha we chose there.
The filmed hyphae are also thicker (10.9 against 9.0~$\mu$m) and less often tree-like (14\% against 36\%, and 26\% inside the hull); diameter and centrality rise together, so the difference in diameter is not a separate bias.
They sit farther from a growing tip than the reference set (3.6 against 1.8~mm), which reverses under the all-node measure (4.4 against 4.8~mm): counting only tips as sources leaves a reference set concentrated near the front.

Within the filmed set itself, speed rises with centrality (Fig.~\ref{fig:bc_selection}C): the bright-field median speed per edge gives a Spearman correlation of 0.39 tipward and 0.37 rootward, falling to 0.21 and 0.25 when distance to the tip is partialled out, and positive in 14 of the 15 sessions tipward and 13 of 14 rootward.
The direction agrees with Oyarte Galvez et al.~\cite{oyarte_galvez_travelling-wave_2025}.
The speeds reported here are therefore those of well-connected hyphae, and the quiet periphery of a colony is not represented.

Two caveats.
Edges along one hypha share almost the same centrality, so correlations pooled over edges overstate how many independent observations there are; the per-session percentiles are the more robust statistic.
Edges are counted one each, not weighted by length.
Taking every node as a source rather than only the growing tips ranks the edges much the same (Spearman 0.78 between the two) and puts the filmed edges at a median 83rd percentile (interquartile range 55--95), but the speed relation is then weaker, 0.18 in both directions.

A single hypha does not carry a single speed: the streaks within one video and one direction spread over about 1~$\mu$m/s, and that spread is itself asymmetric (Fig.~\ref{fig:speed_iqr}).
The rootward stream is the broader of the two almost everywhere along the colony, with a median interquartile range of 1.58 against 0.63~$\mu$m/s tipward.
Only the bright-field videos are used here, because the NileRed streaks are not detected above about 5~$\mu$m/s (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:fl_detection_limit}) and their spread would be cut by that limit rather than by the flow.

\namedsubsection{Growth-induced mass flow}{subsec:si_mass_flow}
% SUGGESTED (20 Oct, Simon): Coco (ideas; graph extraction pipelines): resolve the blue comments and finish the section; figure replaced 20 Oct (figS_mass_flow).
% NOTE (12 Oct): This section was moved from the main text (Results, 'Lipid transport across the network') as it stood, with the outline headings dropped and the first paragraph uncommented; its notes came with it. Its figure references (Fig 4D, Fig 1F,G, SI carbonflow b, d, e) are stale; see the notes.
Hyphal extension as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}D, in addition to consuming carbon, is also associated with the creation of new network volume.
The corresponding demand for water has been shown to be the mechanism driving flows in colonies of other (non-mycorrhizal) fungal species~\cite{heaton_growth-induced_2010, heaton_advection_2012}.
In those systems, the movement of matter within the network is well explained by the movement of water in a network of pipes with set sources and sinks. A hydraulic pressure gradient is established across the network, from inoculation roots to the tips, and the resulting flows are sufficient to explain the observed movement of matter.
We wanted to investigate whether this explanation was compatible with our AM fungal system.
Because volume creation and carbon demand are proportional, we use the same approach as for carbon, simply replacing the magnitude of the carbon source and sink, $\Delta C_k$, with the volume $\Delta V_k$.
We keep a similar global source node corresponding to a water reservoir. {\color{blue} the word ``similar'' suggests that a reader will know the other work which should not be assumed}

We represented the growing network as a temporally evolving graph where each edge has an activation time corresponding to its first appearance.
We call $N$ the set of all graph nodes, and $E$ the set of all graph edges.
This labelling allowed the growth between two time steps to be established in a spatially resolved manner (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic_model}D).
For each growing node $k\in N$ we could establish the total biovolume {\color{blue} maybe ``network volume'' since you have not used the term biovolume above} newly built from that node $\Delta V_k$.
The demand for carbon at each node $\Delta C_k$ is proportional to the newly built biovolume. {\color{blue} maybe just write: The nodal demand for carbon  $\Delta C_k\propto\Delta V_k $
} These are modeled as lipid effluxes out of the system, so the demand gets a negative sign. Lipids must be supplied from the root compartment, which is represented by adding one additional ``source'' node to the network, $S_{source} = - \sum_{k \in N} \Delta C_k$. The ``source'' node is connected to the nodes of the network closest to the plant (see Methods) through zero length junctions. Inevitably, because of loops created through anastomoses, there are multiple possible paths from the ``source'' node to a node with non-zero carbon demand. 

These multiple paths require us to resolve ambiguities in the lipid distribution inside the network, while adhering to the growth constraints in the observed data.
We assume, as in previous work~\cite{heaton_growth-induced_2010}, that carbon flux would be distributed over those paths in a way that minimizes the dissipation of hydraulic energy. 

We assign to each edge $(i,j)\in E$ a hydraulic resistance $\mathcal{R}_{i,j}$ associated with energy dissipation. {\color{blue} i think in network literature these are called resistances, so maybe write as ${\cal R}_{i,j}$, which are different than energy dissipation} We set $\mathcal{R}_{i,j} = \frac{8 \mu L_{i,j}}{\pi b_{i,j}^4}$ where $L_{i,j}$ is the length of the edge, $b_{i,j}$ is its radius, and $\mu$ is a viscosity that is treated as a constant in global energy minimization.
With a carbon flux from $i$ to $j$ $\phi_{i,j}$ the power dissipation {\color{blue} maybe write as: the rate of energy consumption} is set to $\mathcal{R}_{i,j} \phi_{i,j}^2$.
We minimize the objective function $\sum_{(i,j)\in E} \mathcal{R}_{i,j} \phi_{i,j}^2$ under the constraint that for all nodes $k$, $\sum_{i\in N} \phi_{i,k} = \Delta C_k$ (Methods).
This model allows us to estimate, in each edge of the network, the expected direction and lower bound of lipid flux required for the observed growth of the tip (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:carbonflow}A). 

% When comparing these expected lipid fluxes with the one observed in each edge. We found that in 70\% of the cases, the direction of lipid flux could be predicted by our model. This percentage increased to more than 80\% when we considered only points in the network where both the modelled and measured absolute flux were high (Fig. \ref{fig:carbonflow}c).

The calculation  yields the exact volumetric flow rate $Q_{i,j}$ in the hydraulic equivalent of the network.
The speed $v_{i,j}$ was then set as $v_{i,j}=\frac{Q_{i,j}}{\pi b_{i,j}^2}$. {\color{blue} something is wrong with this sentence since model is referred to twice:} We found that this model did not explain the speed in the direction predicted by the model (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:carbonflow}C).
Such a model also does not explain the existence of fluid movement in the other direction.
Indeed, the fluid flow always follows the pressure gradient and therefore cannot be bidirectional.

Finally, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:hyphal_profiling}D and~\ref{fig:asymmetries}A, the flow speed in the modeled and observed direction of the lipid flux narrowly peaks around the value $v_0 = 3\mu m/s$.
% NOTE (12 Oct): Fig 1 has panels A-E, so 'Fig. hyphal_profiling F,G' points to nothing; the tipward speed distribution is Fig 2A/B. The motor-speed range (2-6 um/s, howard_mechanics_2002) repeats the hypothesis section (steinberg_cell_2017).
In addition to not being able to predict a speed of the right order of magnitude, the growth induced mass flow model does not yield such a peaked distribution (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:carbonflow}B).
The peaked distribution instead strongly suggests that the movement of lipids within the AM fungal colony is an active process driven by the movement of molecular motors on tracks walking at fixed speed.
In fact, the speed of known molecular motors can typically be in the range $2-6\mu m/s$ for the faster ones~\cite{howard_mechanics_2002}.
This hypothesis is further motivated by the qualitative observation that the movement of lipids in the colony follows well established tracks.

\namedsubsection{Modelling the tipward speed}{subsec:si_forward_flow}
% NOTE (15 Oct): The text below was drafted by Claude on Simon's request (fill out the forward model SI section), from the notes of this section and the current fits (transport_paper feat/flow-regime: forward_speed_bayes.py, edges.csv / posterior.csv / width_compare.csv; width_feasibility_stats.csv). The width numbers marked 873 edges are from the walls_v4 rebuild (note of 11 Oct below); the force-balance sentence needs Simon's check of the model's form.
The transport model needs the tipward (motor) speed on every edge of the network (Methods), so we asked which properties of a hypha predict it.
We used the bright-field videos only (\nameref{subsec:si_forward_choices}).
For each edge we pooled the moving tipward streaks of all its videos, leaving out video directions with fewer than 5 streaks and video edges shorter than 20~$\mu$m, which gave 1,693 edges with 311,149 streaks in 15 sessions on 5 plates.

% NOTE C15 (30 Oct, per-video measurements, transport_paper PR #167): question. This to-tip fit (forward_speed_bayes) still pools the streaks of all videos on an edge, which the model tables no longer do. Switch it to one value per video as well?

The tipward speed is lower close to the growing tips and levels off within a few millimetres (Fig.~\ref{fig:asymmetries}B).
We described this with a falloff to an offset,
\[
v(d) = v_\infty - (v_\infty - v_{\mathrm{tip}})\, e^{-d/\ell},
\]
with $d$ the network distance to the nearest growing tip at the session.
We fitted it with a hierarchical Bayesian model on the log median streak speed of each edge, $y_i = \log v(d_i) + w_p + u_i + \epsilon_i$, with an edge effect $u_i \sim N(0, \sigma_{\mathrm{edge}})$, a plate effect $w_p$ (summing to zero over plates) and a sampling error $\epsilon_i$ whose standard deviation was bootstrapped from the edge's own streaks, so that edges with few or scattered streaks count less.
Priors were uniform on $v_\infty$ and $v_{\mathrm{tip}}$ (0.05 to 10~$\mu$m/s) and on $\log \ell$ (10~$\mu$m to 50~mm), half-normal on the standard deviations, and the posterior was sampled with adaptive Metropolis (4 chains, $\hat{R} \leq 1.01$).
The posterior medians [95\% intervals] were $v_{\mathrm{tip}} = 2.30$ [2.08, 2.49]~$\mu$m/s, $v_\infty = 3.10$ [2.99, 3.31]~$\mu$m/s and $\ell = 2.98$ [1.70, 6.32]~mm.
Edges scatter around the curve by 0.24 on the log scale (about 27\%), and the plates differ by $-6$ to $+8$\%.
The speed at the tip rests on few edges: 97 lie within 1~mm of a tip and 9 within 250~$\mu$m.
In least-squares fits on the edge medians, fitted on four plates and scored on the fifth, the offset form predicted the held-out edges better than a constant speed or an exponential rise from zero (median fold error 1.14, against 1.17 and 1.16), and a step at a fixed distance did worst.
The transport model uses these posterior medians (Methods).

The tipward speed also rises with the diameter extracted from the same videos (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:width_lumen_speed}), with a within-plate Spearman correlation of 0.54 (1,449 edges).
The relation holds in each of the five plates and is unchanged when the tip distance is accounted for (log-log slope 0.53 [0.46, 0.60], partial within-plate $\rho = 0.53$; 873 edges).
Adding a factor ${(D/9~\mu\mathrm{m})}^\beta$ to the Bayesian fit (1,434 edges with a video-extracted diameter) gave $\beta = 0.49$ [0.45, 0.54] without and $0.47$ [0.43, 0.52] with the tip term; with the diameter included the tip falloff remains ($v_{\mathrm{tip}} = 2.18$, $v_\infty = 2.92~\mu$m/s) and its length shortens to $\ell = 1.10$ [0.63, 1.98]~mm.
Scored on held-out plates, the predictions from tip distance and diameter together, and from a force-balance form with a tip throttle, correlated best with the measured speeds (within-plate $\rho = 0.58$), diameter alone reached 0.50 and tip distance alone 0.22.
The rootward speed rises more steeply with diameter (slope 1.16 [1.05, 1.27]).
Because the network images do not resolve the diameter (\nameref{subsec:si_widths}), the transport model uses the tip distance only (\nameref{subsec:si_forward_choices}).

\namedsubsection{Choices in the tipward speed model}{subsec:si_forward_choices}
% NOTE (15 Oct): The text below was drafted by Claude on Simon's request, from the notes of this section and of the section above.
Three choices shape the tipward speed used in the transport model.

The fits use the bright-field videos (20 frames per second) only.
The NileRed videos (1 or 2 frames per second) lose fast streaks: relative to 1--3~$\mu$m/s, the number of streaks found per bright-field streak drops to 0.22 at 4--5~$\mu$m/s and 0.11 at 6--7~$\mu$m/s at 2 frames per second, and to 0.07--0.08 at 6--8~$\mu$m/s at 1 frame per second, and the per-pixel medians at 1 frame per second level off at 4.4--4.5~$\mu$m/s (Methods, \nameref{subsec:fl_detection_limit}).
Fluorescence speeds are therefore censored near 5~$\mu$m/s, and we do not compare them with the bright-field speeds.

A falloff to zero at the tip, $v(d) = v_\infty (1 - e^{-d/\ell})$, fitted with the same hierarchical Bayesian model, gave $v_\infty = 2.86$ [2.82, 2.90]~$\mu$m/s and $\ell = 219$ [171, 266]~$\mu$m (SI Fig.~\ref{fig:tip_falloff_zero}).
To reach zero at the tip and still pass the edges nearest to it, its falloff length shrinks to about 200~$\mu$m, after which the curve is flat: it reaches 1.9~$\mu$m/s at 250~$\mu$m and 2.8~$\mu$m/s from about 1~mm onwards.
It therefore misses the gradual rise over 1--3~mm and lies about 10\% below the edges beyond 4~mm, while within 500~$\mu$m of a tip it fits as well as the offset form.
By the widely applicable information criterion the offset form predicts better (expected log predictive density higher by 31.3 $\pm$ 8.4), with 8.0 of the difference from the 97 edges within 1~mm of a tip and 19.2 from those beyond 3~mm.
A drop to near zero over the last tens of micrometres, as seen in the hand-traced tip kymographs (\nameref{subsec:si_tips}), is below what the edge data resolve and is not excluded.

Diameter predicts the tipward speed better than tip distance (\nameref{subsec:si_forward_flow}), but the transport model needs a speed on every edge of the network, and there only the network-image diameters are available.
These track the video-extracted diameter poorly (within-plate $\rho$ 0.48--0.61) and correlate with the tipward speed at $\rho$ 0.30--0.36, against 0.54 for the video-extracted diameter (\nameref{subsec:si_widths}).
The transport model therefore uses the tip-distance profile alone, with physical inputs only and no fitted diameter exponent.
A constant motor speed scores worse in the transport model (Methods).

%   - Secomb 2017 (secomb_blood_2017): blood in the microcirculation: red cells migrate to the centreline with a cell-free plasma layer at the wall; in vessels a few cell diameters wide the flow is far from parabolic (Fahraeus-Lindqvist). The closest biological analogue of particle/lumen ~0.1-1.
%   - Abadeh & Lew 2013 (abadeh_mass_2013): in Neurospora hyphae the measured cytoplasmic velocity profile is flat, partial plug flow, attributed to the high organelle density; flow follows imposed pressure gradients. Direct precedent in hyphae.

% NOTE (4 Nov): moved verbatim from the main text (model section); wording to be adapted to this section. Eq.~(\ref{eq:net_inner_flow}) stays in the main text.
By solving the Navier-Stokes equation in the inner pipe, setting the boundary conditions to be $v_m$ at the border of the inner pipe, one can determine the radial speed profile $u(r)$ in cylindrical symmetry to be 

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:speed_profile_main}
u(r) = v_m + \frac{2(q_{w,r<a} - \pi a^2 v_m)(a^2 - r^2)}{\pi a^4}.
\end{equation}

Interestingly, when one assumes $q_w = 0$ as would be the case in a closed pipe system, we have:
\begin{equation}
% NOTE (11 Nov, meeting Coco, agreed: remove capital Theta): write without Theta: u(0) = -v_m [1 + 2 (1-theta)(b^2 - a^2)/a^2]; the sentence 'For all values of Theta and theta' below becomes 'For all theta and a < b'.
\label{eq:max_speed}
u(0) = -v_m\left(1+2\frac{\Theta(1-\theta)}{\theta-\Theta}\right) .
\end{equation}

This result suggests a possible explanation for the bidirectional movement that we observe.
For all values of $\Theta$ and $\theta$, we have $u(0)$ which is of opposite sign compared to $v_m$.
Centerline speed $u(0)$ would be the speed at the center of the pipe, which we do not expect to measure reliably.
% NOTE (4 Nov, outline for Simon): What would a plug conductance look like?
% - A plug in a no-slip pipe moves only by shearing a thin layer of thickness delta at its edge (here between core and condensate); cf. cell-free layer in blood flow.
% - Force balance: Delta p * pi a^2 = tau * 2 pi a L, U = tau delta / mu_l  ->  K_plug = pi a^3 delta / (2 mu_l L), vs K_Pois = pi a^4/(8 mu L).
% - Ratio K_plug/K_Pois = 4 (delta/a)(mu/mu_l): smaller for a thin layer of the same viscosity, comparable or larger if a watery layer lubricates a crowded core.
% - Intermediate cases: power-law (shear-thinning) core with index n gives K ~ a^(3+1/n), from a^4 (Newtonian) to a^3 (plug); Bingham (yield-stress) core = Buckingham-Reiner, K = pi a^4/(8 mu L) [1 - 4/3 (r_p/a) + 1/3 (r_p/a)^4], depends on Delta p, so the network solve becomes nonlinear.
% - Consequence for the model: on tree edges q_w = 0 and q_c = q_s whatever K is; an overall scale on K (mu, delta) changes pressures only, not fluxes; only the a-exponent changes the split in loops (a^3 shifts less backflow into the wider arm than a^4).
% - Earlier test (before theta 0.10; SI tree-and-loop notes, loop arm ratios): K ~ a^3/L gave an SD of the log arm-speed ratio of 0.77 (Poiseuille 0.89, measured 0.53) and loop rho 0.37 (Poiseuille 0.40); a^2/L: SD 0.73, rho 0.29, KS 0.105. % NOTE (2, robustness kappa, definition): kappa is the fewest hyphae that must be cut to disconnect a section of the network (every node within 500 um along the network of an edge) from the root; by Menger's theorem it is the number of hypha-disjoint routes from the section to the root. Cuts count per arc (junction to junction), computed as a maximum flow on the arc graph. A tree section has kappa 1. The imaged network continues below the bottom edge of the image towards the root compartment, so every node within 1 mm of the bottom edge counts as connected to the root (open bottom); with the solver's 13 or so root nodes alone, kappa is 1 lower in median and its relation to speed is inconsistent between plates.

\namedsubsection{Rerouted backflow and network extraction faults}{subsec:si_rerouting}
% NOTE (22 Oct, Simon): moved here from main-text Fig 6F. Point to make: faults in the network extraction let the network-wide pressure solve pool the backflow in the wrong edges, so the model sometimes under-predicts on high-betweenness hyphae. Text below is a first draft; the extraction faults on this stretch are not yet shown (check the graph against the images, and ideally a corrected graph re-solved).
Because the pressure in the model is solved over the whole network, the backflow of a hypha does not have to return through that hypha: where the extracted network offers parallel routes, the solve can send part of it through neighbouring edges.
On a trail of plate 2, session 3, 8 to 19~mm from the root, the model returns only 0.68 (interquartile range 0.44--0.84) of the trail's motor stream through the trail itself and sends the rest back through side hyphae, giving a rootward speed of 2.0~$\mu$m/s against 6.4~$\mu$m/s measured (20 segments; 3.3~$\mu$m/s measured tipward).
% NOTE (2 Nov, plate labels): "trail LeftMiddle of PNF080 at t91" -> "a trail of plate 2, session 3".
In the model the trail's net flux points tipward on 9 to 18~mm: volume enters at about 8.8~mm through a side branch and leaves into side hyphae at 17 to 21~mm, a circulation loop of about 10~mm.
Measured, the rootward speed on this stretch (6.4~$\mu$m/s) exceeds even a full backflow of the trail's own motor stream ($\sim$3.5~$\mu$m/s).
The difference is not explained by growth nearby; routing past demand raises the model only to 2.5~$\mu$m/s, splitting the demand at crossings lowers it to 2.1~$\mu$m/s, and idle motors change nothing.
